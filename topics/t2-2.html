Quadratics, rational, exponential and log models

Guiding questionHow do algebraic parameters control turning points, root counts, and asymptotes across the core families of mathematical models?

What you need to be able to do

You need function notation, domains and inverse functions from 2.1 Lines, functions, domains, inverses and composites, and the laws of exponents and logarithms from 1.2 Exponents, logarithms and the binomial theorem, before you start this page.

  • Convert quadratics between standard form ax^2 + bx + c, vertex form a(x - h)^2 + k (by completing the square), and factored form a(x - p)(x - q).
  • Use the discriminant Delta = b^2 - 4ac to determine the number of real roots or line-curve intersections (Delta > 0, Delta = 0, Delta < 0, Delta >= 0).
  • Solve quadratic inequalities (including parameters inside Delta) by sketching the parabola or drawing a sign diagram.
  • Sketch linear rational functions f(x) = (ax + b)/(cx + d), stating the equations of the vertical asymptote x = -d/c and horizontal asymptote y = a/c.
  • Model growth and decay with f(t) = a e^{kt} + c and sketch logarithmic graphs y = ln(x - h) with their asymptotes and intercepts.
  • Solve transcendental equations f(x) = g(x) on Paper 2 using GDC graph intersections and sketch the curves to earn method marks.

Question decoder: spotting disguised discriminant & modelling cues

Many Paper 1 quadratic questions never use the word “discriminant”—they disguise Delta = b^2 - 4ac behind geometric words like tangent, never meets, or always positive.

Question decoder — Quadratics, rational functions, exponentials & GDC solving
What the exam question says What it means (formula & setup) Paper 1 (No GDC) vs Paper 2 (GDC)
“Two distinct real roots” / “Line intersects the curve twice” Equate f(x) = g(x), rearrange to Ax^2 + Bx + C = 0, and set Delta = B^2 - 4AC > 0. If Delta > 0 gives a quadratic inequality in k, sketch a mini parabola in k to pick the right intervals!
“Touches the x-axis” / “Line is tangent to the parabola” / “Equal (repeated) root” Rearrange to Ax^2 + Bx + C = 0 and set Delta = B^2 - 4AC = 0. Setting Delta = 0 is much faster than using derivatives when an unknown parameter k appears in multiple terms.
“No real roots” / “Line never meets the curve” / “Always positive for all x ∈ ℝ” Set Delta = B^2 - 4AC < 0 (and require leading coefficient a > 0 for “always positive”, or a < 0 for “always negative”). Remember: a parabola lies entirely above the x-axis if and only if it opens upwards (a > 0) and never crosses the axis (Delta < 0).
“The equation has real roots” Could be 1 repeated root or 2 distinct roots: set Delta = b^2 - 4ac >= 0 (non-strict!). Watch the wording carefully: “real roots” (Delta >= 0) vs “two distinct real roots” (Delta > 0).
“Solve ax^2 + bx + c > 0 (or <= 0)” Factor to find the roots p, q, then always sketch the parabola or a sign diagram. Never write x > +-3! For a > 0: < 0 is between the roots (p < x < q); > 0 is outside the roots (x < p or x > q).
“Find the initial population / temperature” Substitute t = 0 into the model and use b^0 = 1 or e^0 = 1. In P(t) = A + B e^{-kt}, the initial value is P(0) = A + B, not A and not B alone!
“Find the long-term / limiting value as t -> infty” Horizontal asymptote of the decay/growth model: since e^{-kt} -> 0 (for k > 0), P(t) -> A. Write “As t -> infty, e^{-kt} -> 0” explicitly if the question says Explain or Show that.
“Solve e^x = 5 - x^2” Mixed exponential + polynomial equation has no algebraic closed form. Use Paper 2 GDC graph intersection! Plot Y_1 = e^x and Y_2 = 5 - x^2 (or Y_1 = e^x + x^2 - 5 zeroes). Draw a quick sketch showing the intersections to secure [M1].
SL 2.6 · SL 2.7

Quadratic forms, completing the square and the discriminant

Every quadratic function f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c (a != 0) graphs as a symmetrical parabola. Depending on what the question gives or asks for, you should pick the most direct of the three quadratic forms:

The three quadratic forms and what each reveals immediately
Form Equation What you can read off immediately
Standard form f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c y-intercept at (0, c); axis of symmetry x = -b / (2a); concavity (a > 0 opens up, a < 0 opens down).
Vertex (completed square) form f(x) = a(x - h)^2 + k Vertex (turning point) at (h, k); axis of symmetry x = h; range is y >= k (if a > 0) or y <= k (if a < 0).
Factored (intercept) form f(x) = a(x - p)(x - q) x-intercepts (roots) at (p, 0) and (q, 0); axis of symmetry halfway between roots at x = (p + q) / 2.

Completing the square when a != 1

To convert ax^2 + bx + c into vertex form a(x - h)^2 + k without making sign errors, factor a out of the first two terms only, halve the coefficient of x, and remember to multiply the subtracted square by a when bringing it outside the bracket:

2x2 − 12x + 11 = 2(x2 − 6x) + 11 = 2[(x − 3)2 − 9] + 11 = 2(x − 3)2 − 7
x = −b/(2a)
shortcut check: h = -(-12)/(2 × 2) = 3 and k = f(3) = 2(9) - 36 + 11 = -7
Δ = b2 − 4ac
discriminant: Delta > 0 (2 distinct real roots), Delta = 0 (1 repeated root), Delta < 0 (no real roots)

In your Formula Booklet (SL 2.6 and SL 2.7): The axis of symmetry x = -b/(2a), the quadratic formula x = (-b +- sqrt(b^2 - 4ac))/(2a) and the discriminant Delta = b^2 - 4ac are printed. What each sign of Delta tells you about the roots is not printed, so learn it.

Solving a disguised discriminant inequality for a tangent/non-intersecting line

Consider the quadratic curve y = x^2 + (k - 2)x + 5 and the straight line y = 2x + 1, where k ∈ ℝ. Find the set of values of k for which the line and the curve never intersect. [5 marks]

  1. Intersections occur where both y-values are equal; standard quadratic form lets us identify A, B, and C.

    x2 + (k − 2)x + 5 = 2x + 1 ⇒ x2 + (k − 4)x + 4 = 0   [M1 A1]

  2. The line and parabola never meet if and only if the intersection equation has no real roots.

    Δ = (k − 4)2 − 4(1)(4) < 0 ⇒ (k − 4)2 − 16 < 0   [M1]

  3. Difference of two squares (or expanding k² − 8k < 0) gives critical values k = 0 and k = 8. Since this quadratic in k opens upwards, it is negative between the roots.

    k(k − 8) < 0 ⇒ critical values k = 0, 8   [(A1)]
    0 < k < 8   [A1]

0 < k < 8

The quadratic function f(x) = 2x^2 - 6x + k has one repeated real root (its graph is tangent to the x-axis). Find the exact value of k.

  1. For a quadratic to have one repeated root (touch the x-axis), set its discriminant Delta = b^2 - 4ac = 0.
  2. Substitute a = 2, b = -6, and c = k into (-6)^2 - 4(2)(k) = 0.
Δ = (−6)2 − 4(2)(k) = 36 − 8k = 0
8k = 36 ⇒ k = 368 = 4.5 (or 9/2)
SL 2.8

Reciprocal and linear rational functions

The reciprocal function f(x) = 1/x (x != 0) has asymptotes x = 0 and y = 0. It is self-inverse: f(f(x)) = 1/(1/x) = x, so f^{-1}(x) = f(x), and its graph is symmetric in the line y = x.

A linear-over-linear rational function has the form f(x) = (ax + b)/(cx + d) with c != 0 and ad - bc != 0 (otherwise f is a constant with a hole). Its graph is a transformation of the basic reciprocal hyperbola y = 1/x and is anchored by two perpendicular asymptotes:

f(x) = ax + bcx + d = A + Bcx + d  ⇒  VA: x = −dc,   HA: y = ac
Vertical asymptote
set denominator cx + d = 0 to get the line x = -d/c (excluded from domain)
Horizontal asymptote
ratio of leading x-coefficients as x -> +-infty gives the line y = a/c (excluded from range)
Axial intercepts
y-intercept at x = 0 gives (0, b/d); x-intercept at y = 0 (ax + b = 0) gives (-b/a, 0)

Common mistake — Writing an asymptote as a number instead of an equation: Writing “Vertical asymptote = 2” or “HA = 3” usually loses the mark: markschemes expect an equation. An asymptote is a straight line, so you must write the full equation x = 2 for a vertical asymptote and y = 3 for a horizontal asymptote.

Rewriting a rational function to identify asymptotes and intercepts

The function f is defined by f(x) = (3x - 5)/(x - 2) for x != 2.
(a) State the equations of the vertical and horizontal asymptotes of the graph of f. [2 marks]
(b) Express f(x) in the form A + B/(x - 2), where A, B ∈ ℤ. [2 marks]

  1. Set the denominator to 0 for the vertical asymptote, and take the ratio of x-coefficients for the horizontal asymptote.

    Vertical asymptote: x = 2   [A1]
    Horizontal asymptote: y = 3   (from 3x / x)   [A1]

  2. Creating 3(x − 2) in the numerator lets (x − 2) cancel cleanly into the constant horizontal asymptote value A = 3.

    3x − 5x − 2 = 3(x − 2) + 6 − 5x − 2 = 3 + 1x − 2   (A = 3, B = 1)   [M1 A1]

(a) VA: x = 2, HA: y = 3; (b) 3 + 1/(x - 2)

The graph of f(x) = (4x + 7)/(2x + 5) has a vertical asymptote with equation x = k. Find the exact value of k.

  1. A vertical asymptote occurs where the denominator equals zero (provided the numerator is non-zero there).
  2. Solve 2x + 5 = 0 for x.
2x + 5 = 0 ⇒ 2x = −5 ⇒ k = −2.5 (or −5/2)

Exponential models, logarithmic graphs and GDC equation solving

Exponential and logarithmic functions are inverse pairs: y = a^x and y = log_a x (and in particular y = e^x and y = ln x) are reflections of each other across the line y = x.

In your Formula Booklet (SL 2.9): a^x = e^{x ln a} and log_a a^x = x = a^{log_a x} (for a > 0, a != 1, and x > 0 in the second form) are printed. Use the first to rewrite any exponential with base e, for example 2^x = e^{x ln 2}.

Comparing shifted exponential and logarithmic graphs
Feature Exponential model f(t) = A e^{kt} + C Logarithmic model g(x) = a ln(x - h) + d
Domain All real numbers t ∈ ℝ (or t >= 0 in time contexts) Restricted: x - h > 0 => x > h
Range Restricted: y > C (if A > 0) or y < C (if A < 0) All real numbers y ∈ ℝ
Asymptote Horizontal asymptote y = C (since e^{kt} -> 0 as kt -> -infty) Vertical asymptote x = h (where the log argument hits 0)
Initial / key point At t = 0, f(0) = A e^0 + C = A + C When x - h = 1 (x = h + 1), ln(1) = 0 so g(h + 1) = d

Exam tip — Earning full marks on Paper 2 GDC intersection questions: When a Paper 2 question asks you to solve an equation like e^{-0.4x} = x^2 - 3 where x appears both inside an exponential/log and inside a polynomial, do not try to isolate x algebraically—it is impossible! Instead, graph Y_1 = e^{-0.4x} and Y_2 = x^2 - 3 on your GDC, draw a small labelled sketch showing the curves and their intersection point(s) in your answer booklet (this secures the [M1] method mark even if you misread a digit), and write the coordinates to 3 s.f.

Modelling Newton's cooling with an exponential decay function

The temperature T (in °C) of a cup of coffee t minutes after it is poured is modelled by T(t) = 22 + 68 e^{-0.08t} for t >= 0.
(a) Write down the initial temperature of the coffee and the room temperature it approaches after a very long time. [2 marks]
(b) Find the exact time t when the coffee reaches 56 °C, giving your answer in the form a ln 2. [3 marks]

  1. At t = 0, e⁰ = 1; as t → ∞, the exponential term tends to 0, leaving the horizontal asymptote T = 22.

    Initial temperature: T(0) = 22 + 68(1) = 90 °C   [A1]
    Long-term room temperature (as t → ∞): 22 °C   [A1]

  2. Always subtract 22 and divide by 68 BEFORE applying ln to both sides.

    22 + 68e−0.08t = 56 ⇒ 68e−0.08t = 34 ⇒ e−0.08t = 3468 = 12   [M1 A1]

  3. Writing ln(1/2) = −ln 2 cancels the negative sign and gives the exact form a ln 2.

    −0.08t = ln(1/2) = −ln 2 ⇒ t = ln 20.08 = 12.5 ln 2 (or 252 ln 2) minutes   [A1]

(a) Initial: 90 °C, Long-term: 22 °C; (b) t = 12.5 ln 2 minutes

Using your GDC, find the positive root of the equation e^x = 6 - x^2, giving your answer correct to 3 significant figures.

  1. Plot Y_1 = e^x and Y_2 = 6 - x^2 on your GDC (or find the positive zero of f(x) = e^x + x^2 - 6).
  2. Use the GDC intersection tool in the first quadrant (x > 0) and round x ≈ 1.39778 to 3 significant figures.
Intersecting y = ex and y = 6 − x2 for x > 0 gives (1.398, 4.046) ⇒ x = 1.40 (3 s.f.).
