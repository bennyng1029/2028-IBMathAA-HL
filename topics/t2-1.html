Topic 2.1 · SL 2.1–2.5

Lines, functions, domains, inverses and composites

Guiding questionHow do mathematical rules map allowed inputs to outputs, when can we chain two rules together, and when can a rule be reversed?

What you need to be able to do

  • Write and convert equations of straight lines between gradient-intercept form y = mx + c, point-gradient form y - y_1 = m(x - x_1), and general form ax + by + d = 0.
  • Use parallel (m_1 = m_2) and perpendicular (m_1 m_2 = -1) gradients to find equations of normals and perpendicular bisectors.
  • Find the natural domain and exact range of a function by checking denominators, even roots, logarithms, turning points, and asymptotes.
  • Evaluate and simplify composite functions (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)) and test whether Range(g) fits inside Domain(f).
  • Find the inverse function f^{-1}(x) algebraically, state its domain and range by swapping with f, and sketch y = f^{-1}(x) as a reflection in y = x.
  • Solve f(x) = f^{-1}(x) efficiently by solving f(x) = x along the mirror line y = x (valid when f is increasing).

Question decoder: spotting which technique to use

Topic 2 exam questions use short, standard phrases that point to a specific algebraic shortcut. Use this table to translate the wording before you start writing.

“Find the perpendicular bisector of [AB]” Line through the midpoint M((x_1+x_2)/2, (y_1+y_2)/2) with negative reciprocal gradient m_2 = -1 / m_1. Find M and m_{AB} first, flip and negate to get m_2, then plug into y - y_M = m_2(x - x_M). Clear fractions if ax + by + d = 0 is requested.
“State the largest possible domain of f” Check the 3 domain exclusions: denominator != 0, inside even root >= 0, and inside log > 0. P1: Solve the resulting linear or quadratic inequality. P2: Graph y = f(x) to confirm where the curve exists and where vertical asymptotes sit.
“Find the range of f on a <= x <= b” Set of all y-values reached on the given domain. Do NOT just plug in the endpoints! Always sketch the graph on [a, b]. Check both endpoints f(a), f(b) and any local maximum/minimum (vertex) inside [a, b].
“Find (f ∘ g)(x)” Work inside-out: (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)). Replace every x in the formula for f with the entire expression g(x). If asked for a single number (f ∘ g)(2), calculate the number g(2) first, then plug that number into f.
“For (f ∘ g)(x) to exist / be defined…” Every output of the inner function must be a legal input for the outer function: Range(g) must be a subset of Domain(f). Find Range(g) first, then restrict the domain of g (or adjust a parameter) so that every output satisfies Domain(f).
“Find f^{-1}(a)” (where a is a number) An input to f^{-1} is an output of f. Do not invert a messy function—just solve f(x) = a! P1: Solve f(x) = a algebraically and keep the root inside Domain(f). P2: Intersect y = f(x) with the horizontal line y = a.
“Solve f(x) = f^{-1}(x)” A curve and its reflection in y = x meet on the mirror line itself (for increasing functions). Solve f(x) = x instead! Instead of equating a quadratic to a square root (which creates a degree-4 equation), solve the simple equation f(x) = x inside the restricted domain.
SL 2.1

Equations of straight lines and perpendicular bisectors

A straight line in the coordinate plane is completely determined by its steepness—called its gradient m—and a single point on the line. Your Formula Booklet gives three equivalent ways to write the equation of a line.

m = y2 − y1x2 − x1  ·  y = mx + c  ·  y − y1 = m(x − x1)  ·  ax + by + d = 0
m
gradient of the line (x_2 != x_1)
c
y-intercept (where the line crosses the y-axis at (0, c))
(x1, y1)
any known point lying on the line
a, b, d
constants in general form (usually integers; any non-zero multiple of the equation is equivalent unless the question says otherwise)

In your Formula Booklet (SL 2.1): The gradient formula m = (y_2 - y_1)/(x_2 - x_1) and all three line equations are printed. You must memorize the parallel and perpendicular gradient relationships (the midpoint formula is in the booklet under SL 3.1).

Parallel lines, perpendicular lines and perpendicular bisectors

  • Parallel lines never intersect and have the same gradient: m_1 = m_2.
  • Perpendicular lines meet at 90°. Their gradients are negative reciprocals of each other: m_1 m_2 = -1, so m_2 = -1 / m_1. (A horizontal line y = k has gradient 0 and is perpendicular to a vertical line x = h, whose gradient is undefined.)
  • The perpendicular bisector of a line segment [AB] is the set of all points equidistant from A and B. To find its equation in three steps:
    1. Find the midpoint M = ((x_1 + x_2)/2, (y_1 + y_2)/2).
    2. Find the segment gradient m_{AB}, then flip and change the sign to get the perpendicular gradient m_{perp} = -1 / m_{AB}.
    3. Substitute M and m_{perp} into point-gradient form y - y_M = m_{perp}(x - x_M).

Exam tip — Check the requested format: If a question says “Give your answer in the form ax + by + d = 0 where a, b, d ∈ ℤ”, leaving your answer as y = (2/3)x - 5/3 loses the final [A1] mark. Multiply the entire equation by the common denominator (3) and move all terms to one side: 2x - 3y - 5 = 0.

Finding the perpendicular bisector in general integer form

Points A(-2, 7) and B(4, 3) lie in the Cartesian plane. Find the equation of the perpendicular bisector of [AB], giving your answer in the form ax + by + d = 0 where a, b, d are integers. [4 marks]

  1. The bisector cuts segment [AB] in half, so it must pass through the average of the x- and y-coordinates.

    M = (−2 + 42, 7 + 32) = (1, 5)   [A1]

  2. Because the bisector meets [AB] at 90°, its gradient is the negative reciprocal of the gradient of [AB].

    mAB = 3 − 74 − (−2) = −46 = −23   [(A1)]
    m⊥ = −1−2/3 = 32   [M1]

  3. Substitute M(1, 5) and m = 3/2 into y − y₁ = m(x − x₁), then multiply by 2 to clear fractions.

    y − 5 = 32(x − 1) ⇒ 2y − 10 = 3x − 3 ⇒ 3x − 2y + 7 = 0   [A1]

3x - 2y + 7 = 0 (or -3x + 2y - 7 = 0)

The perpendicular bisector of the segment joining P(1, 2) and Q(5, 10) crosses the y-axis at (0, c). Find the exact value of c.

  1. Find the midpoint M of P(1, 2) and Q(5, 10), and the gradient m_{PQ} = (10 - 2)/(5 - 1).
  2. You should get M(3, 6) and m_{PQ} = 2, so the perpendicular gradient is m_{perp} = -1/2. Substitute x = 0 into y - 6 = (-1/2)(x - 3).
M = (3, 6) and mPQ = 10 − 25 − 1 = 2 ⇒ m⊥ = −12
y − 6 = −12(x − 3) ⇒ at x = 0, c = 6 + 32 = 7.5
SL 2.2 · SL 2.3 · SL 2.4

Functions, natural domains, ranges and key graph features

A function f is a rule that takes an input x from a set of allowed inputs—the domain—and produces exactly one output y = f(x). The set of all actual outputs produced is the range.

  • Vertical line test (Is it a function?): A graph represents a function if and only if every vertical line x = k hits the curve at most once. A circle x^2 + y^2 = 9 fails the vertical line test because x = 0 gives two outputs (y = 3 and y = -3).
  • Horizontal line test (Is it one-to-one?): A function is one-to-one if every horizontal line y = k hits the curve at most once. Only one-to-one functions have an inverse function on their full domain.

The three natural domain killers

When a question asks for the natural domain (or “largest possible domain in ℝ”) without specifying a restriction, start with all real numbers x ∈ ℝ and remove any x that breaks one of these three algebraic rules:

  1. No division by zero: For 1 / g(x), require g(x) != 0.
  2. No negative numbers inside an even root: For sqrt(g(x)), require g(x) >= 0. (If the square root is in a denominator, 1 / sqrt(g(x)), both rules combine to g(x) > 0 strictly!)
  3. Only strictly positive numbers inside a logarithm: For ln(g(x)) or log_a(g(x)), require g(x) > 0.

Common mistake — Finding the range by plugging in only the endpoints: Suppose f(x) = (x - 2)^2 + 1 on the domain 0 <= x <= 5. If you only compute f(0) = 5 and f(5) = 10, you might write that the range is 5 <= y <= 10. That is wrong! At x = 2 (which lies inside [0, 5]), the parabola dips down to its vertex (2, 1). The true range is 1 <= y <= 10. Always sketch the curve on the given domain before stating the range.

Finding the natural domain and exact range of a transformed function

Consider the function f(x) = 5 - sqrt(2x + 6). State the largest possible domain of f and find the corresponding range. [4 marks]

  1. A real square root is defined if and only if its radicand is non-negative.

    2x + 6 ≥ 0 ⇒ 2x ≥ −6 ⇒ x ≥ −3   [M1 A1]

  2. The principal square root √(2x + 6) takes every value from 0 upwards as x increases from −3 to ∞.

    Since sqrt(2x + 6) >= 0 for all x >= -3, multiplying by −1 and adding 5 gives:   [M1]
    5 − sqrt(2x + 6) <= 5 ⇒ f(x) ≤ 5 (or y ∈ (-infty, 5])   [A1]

Domain: x >= -3; Range: f(x) <= 5

The function g(x) = (x - 3)^2 + 2 is defined on the restricted domain 1 <= x <= 7. Find the minimum value in the range of g.

  1. Identify the vertex of the parabola y = (x - 3)^2 + 2 and check whether its x-coordinate lies inside 1 <= x <= 7.
  2. Since x = 3 is inside [1, 7] and (x - 3)^2 >= 0, the minimum output occurs at the vertex x = 3.
At the endpoints: g(1) = (−2)2 + 2 = 6 and g(7) = 42 + 2 = 18.
However, the vertex at x = 3 lies inside [1, 7], giving minimum value g(3) = 0 + 2 = 2 (so the range is 2 <= y <= 18).

Sketching graphs and graphing with technology

A sketch shows the shape of a graph and its key features, labelled with coordinates or equations: axis intercepts, turning points, asymptotes and the end points of a restricted domain. It does not need to be to scale. To draw a graph means to plot it accurately on given axes. When you transfer a graph from your GDC screen to paper, use the same window (mark the x- and y-range on your axes), keep the shape, and write the coordinates the GDC gives next to each feature.

Your GDC can also graph sums and differences of functions: enter Y_1 and Y_2, then Y_3 = Y_1 + Y_2 or Y_3 = Y_1 - Y_2. The zeros of Y_1 - Y_2 are the x-coordinates where the graphs of Y_1 and Y_2 intersect.

Composite functions (f ∘ g)(x) and inverse functions f^{-1}(x)

When the output of one function g is fed directly as the input into a second function f, we form the composite function (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)). Read (f ∘ g)(x) from the inside out: g acts on x first, and f acts on g(x) second.

(f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x))  ·  (f ∘ f−1)(x) = (f−1 ∘ f)(x) = x
g(x)
inner function (applied first to input x)
f(…)
outer function (applied second to the output g(x))
Existence rule
Range(g) must be a subset of Domain(f)
f−1(x)
inverse function (reverses f; exists only when f is one-to-one)

Inverse functions and the mirror line y = x

The inverse function f^{-1} undoes what f does: if f maps a -> b, then f^{-1} maps b -> a. Because every (x, y) coordinate pair swaps to (y, x), three powerful facts always hold:

  1. Algebraic recipe: Write y = f(x), swap x and y, and solve for y in terms of x. If a +-sqrt(...) appears, use the restricted domain of f (which becomes the range of f^{-1}) to pick the correct sign!
  2. Domain and range swap: Domain(f^{-1}) = Range(f) and Range(f^{-1}) = Domain(f). If an exam question asks for the domain of f^{-1}, just find the range of f.
  3. Geometric reflection: The graph of y = f^{-1}(x) is the reflection of y = f(x) across the diagonal line y = x. In particular, any intersection of an increasing function y = f(x) with y = f^{-1}(x) lies directly on the line y = x, so you can solve f(x) = f^{-1}(x) by solving f(x) = x!

Common mistake — Confusing f^{-1}(x) with the reciprocal 1 / f(x): In function notation, the superscript -1 in f^{-1}(x) always means the inverse function (reversing input and output), never the reciprocal (f(x))^{-1} = 1 / f(x).

Inverting a domain-restricted quadratic and solving f(x) = f⁻¹(x)

The function f is defined by f(x) = (x - 3)^2 - 2 for x <= 3.
(a) Find an expression for f^{-1}(x) and state its domain. [4 marks]
(b) Solve the equation f(x) = f^{-1}(x). [4 marks]

  1. The vertex is at (3, −2) and the parabola opens upwards, so f(x) ≥ −2 for all x ≤ 3.

    Range of f is y ≥ −2 ⇒ Domain of f−1 is x ≥ −2   [A1]

  2. Because Domain(f) is x ≤ 3, Range(f⁻¹) must be y ≤ 3, which forces the negative square root.

    x = (y − 3)2 − 2 ⇒ (y − 3)2 = x + 2 ⇒ y = 3 ± sqrt(x + 2)   [M1]
    Since y ≤ 3, choose the minus sign: f−1(x) = 3 − sqrt(x + 2)   [R1 A1]

  3. Equating f(x) to x avoids squaring radicals. Only roots inside Domain(f) ∩ Domain(f⁻¹), namely −2 ≤ x ≤ 3, are valid. Because f is decreasing, f and f⁻¹ could also meet off the line y = x, so that must be checked too.

    (x − 3)2 − 2 = x ⇒ x2 − 6x + 9 − 2 − x = 0 ⇒ x2 − 7x + 7 = 0   [M1]
    x = 7 ± sqrt(49 − 28)2 = 7 ± sqrt(21)2   [A1]
    Since sqrt(21) ≈ 4.58, (7 + sqrt(21))/2 ≈ 5.79 > 3 (reject). Thus x = 7 − sqrt(21)2   [A1]
    Off the mirror line, f(f(x)) = x also gives the pair x = (5 - sqrt(17))/2 ≈ 0.438, f(x) ≈ 4.56; since 4.56 > 3 is not in Domain(f), it is rejected, so the only intersection lies on y = x   [R1]

(a) f^{-1}(x) = 3 - sqrt(x + 2) for x >= -2; (b) x = (7 - sqrt(21))/2

Let f(x) = 2x + 3 and g(x) = x^2 - 4. Solve the equation (f ∘ g)(x) = -0.5 for x > 0.

  1. Form the composite function from the inside out: f(g(x)) = 2(x^2 - 4) + 3 = 2x^2 - 5.
  2. Set 2x^2 - 5 = -0.5, solve for x^2 = 2.25, and take the positive square root.
(f ∘ g)(x) = 2(x2 − 4) + 3 = 2x2 − 5 = −0.5
2x2 = 4.5 ⇒ x2 = 2.25 ⇒ x = 1.5 (since x > 0)

Not needed for IB: You do not need formal university set-theory proofs of surjectivity onto an arbitrary codomain distinct from the range, nor epsilon-delta continuity proofs. In IB Math AA HL, a function is invertible if and only if it is one-to-one on its domain (passes the horizontal line test), with its codomain taken to be its range.
