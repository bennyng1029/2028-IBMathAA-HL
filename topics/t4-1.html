Topic 4.1 · SL 4.1–4.4, 4.10

Descriptive statistics, correlation and regression

Guiding questionHow do we summarize center, spread, and linear relationships in noisy data—and why does the direction of prediction change the regression line we must use?

What you need to be able to do

  • Distinguish population vs sample and discrete vs continuous data, and compare simple random, systematic, stratified, quota, and convenience sampling.
  • Interpret histograms, cumulative frequency graphs, and box-and-whisker plots, compare two distributions using box plots, and test formally for outliers using [Q_1 - 1.5 IQR, Q_3 + 1.5 IQR].
  • Estimate the mean x̄ and population standard deviation σ from grouped frequency tables using mid-interval values.
  • Find the new mean, variance, standard deviation, and IQR after linear coding Y = aX + b (ȳ = a x̄ + b, σ_Y = |a|σ_X).
  • Find Pearson's correlation coefficient r and the regression line from raw data on a GDC, interpret r and the parameters a and b in context, and judge whether a prediction is reliable using correlation strength and interpolation vs extrapolation.
  • Choose between the regression line of y on x (y = ax + b) and x on y (x = cy + d), and use their intersection at the mean point (x̄, ȳ).

Question decoder: spotting which technique to use

Topic 4.1 questions test whether you know which summary measure, outlier rule, or regression line matches the wording of the prompt. Use this table to translate exam phrases directly into your working.

Question decoder — Descriptive statistics, correlation and regression
What the exam question says What it means (formula & setup) Paper 1 (No GDC) vs Paper 2 (GDC)
“Estimate y when x = x_0” Use the regression line of y on x: y = ax + b (minimizes vertical squared residuals). Substitute x = x_0 into y = ax + b. On GDC, run LinReg(ax+b) with Xlist = L1, Ylist = L2.
“Estimate x when y = y_0” (when neither variable is controlled) Use the regression line of x on y: x = cy + d (minimizes horizontal squared residuals). Never rearrange y = ax + b to solve for x! On GDC, swap the lists in LinReg(ax+b): set Xlist = L2 (y), Ylist = L1 (x).
“Given both regression lines y = ax + b and x = cy + d, find x̄ and ȳ” Both regression lines always intersect at the mean point (x̄, ȳ). Solve the two linear equations simultaneously for x and y (by substitution on Paper 1, or graph/system solver on Paper 2).
“Determine whether v is an outlier” Compute IQR = Q_3 - Q_1, lower fence Q_1 - 1.5 IQR, and upper fence Q_3 + 1.5 IQR. Write down the numerical value of the relevant fence and explicitly compare v < Q_1 - 1.5 IQR or v > Q_3 + 1.5 IQR for the reasoning mark [R1].
“Comment on the validity / reliability of your estimate” Check two conditions: 1. Is |r| close to 1? 2. Is the input inside the observed data range (interpolation) or outside it (extrapolation)? If the input lies outside [x_{min}, x_{max}], state: “Unreliable because it requires extrapolation outside the range of the data.”
“Each data value x is transformed to y = ax + b” Mean/median/quartiles scale and shift: ȳ = a x̄ + b (if a < 0, Q_1 and Q_3 swap). Spread measures only scale by |a|: σ_y = |a|σ_x, σ_y^2 = a^2 σ_x^2. Adding b has zero effect on standard deviation, variance, or IQR. If a < 0, use |a| (the standard deviation never becomes negative).

What is NOT in the IB Math AA HL syllabus: Hypothesis testing (χ^2 tests of independence, χ^2 goodness-of-fit, t-tests, z-tests), Spearman's rank correlation r_s and unbiased sample variance s_{n-1}^2 belong to IB Math AI. Calculating Pearson's r or regression slopes by hand from summation formulas is not required in AA: use your GDC. In IB Math AA, you always find r, σ_x, and regression equations directly from your GDC or from summary clues.

SL 4.1–4.2

Sampling methods, cumulative frequency and box plots

A population is the entire group you want to describe, whereas a sample is a smaller subset you actually measure. Data can be discrete (counted in exact steps, such as number of siblings) or continuous (measured on a continuous scale, such as height or reaction time).

Comparison of the five IB sampling techniques (SL 4.1)
Sampling method How the sample is chosen Key strength & main source of bias
Simple random Every member of the population (and every subset of size n) has an equal probability of being chosen, using a random number generator. Unbiased in expectation, but requires a complete list (sampling frame) of the entire population.
Systematic Pick a random starting point among the first k items, then select every k-th member (k = N / n) along an ordered list. Fast and evenly spread, but biased if the list has a hidden periodic cycle matching k.
Stratified Divide the population into distinct subgroups (strata) and take a simple random sample from each stratum proportional to its size: n_i = (N_i / N) × n. Guarantees proportional representation of every subgroup; still requires a full list within each stratum.
Quota Divide the population into subgroups like stratified sampling, but select subjects non-randomly (by convenience) until each subgroup quota is filled. No population list needed, but prone to interviewer selection bias.
Convenience Poll whichever individuals are easiest to reach first (e.g., the first 20 students walking into the library). Quickest method, but severely biased because the sample rarely represents the whole population.

Cumulative frequency graphs, quartiles and formal outlier fences

For grouped continuous data, a histogram plots class intervals on the horizontal axis and frequency on the vertical axis (with no gaps between bars). A cumulative frequency graph plots the running total of frequencies against the upper boundary of each class interval.

From a cumulative frequency curve of n total observations, read off horizontal lines at:

  • Lower quartile Q_1: 25% of n (0.25n)
  • Median M (or Q_2): 50% of n (0.50n)
  • Upper quartile Q_3: 75% of n (0.75n)
IQR = Q3 − Q1    |    Lower fence = Q1 − 1.5 × IQR    |    Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR
IQR
interquartile range (spread of the middle 50% of data)
Q1, Q3
lower quartile (25th percentile) and upper quartile (75th percentile)
Outlier
any data value strictly below the lower fence or strictly above the upper fence

Formula Booklet check (SL 4.2): IQR = Q_3 - Q_1 is in your Formula Booklet, but the outlier fence multipliers Q_1 - 1.5 × IQR and Q_3 + 1.5 × IQR are not printed in the booklet—memorize the 1.5 × IQR rule!

Testing for outliers and adjusting box-plot whiskers

The reaction times (in milliseconds) of 15 athletes have minimum 142, lower quartile Q_1 = 180, median 196, upper quartile Q_3 = 212, and two largest values 254 and 268. Determine which, if any, of the extreme values are outliers, and state the endpoints of the two whiskers on a box-and-whisker plot. [5 marks]

  1. The outlier fences depend on IQR = Q₃ − Q₁.

    IQR = Q3 − Q1 = 212 − 180 = 32 [A1]

  2. Any value below Q₁ − 1.5(IQR) or above Q₃ + 1.5(IQR) is classified as an outlier.

    1.5 × IQR = 1.5 × 32 = 48
    Lower fence = 180 − 48 = 132, Upper fence = 212 + 48 = 260 [M1 A1]

  3. Whiskers extend to the most extreme data points that are NOT outliers.

    Since 142 ≥ 132, the minimum 142 is not a lower outlier (left whisker ends at 142). [R1]
    Since 268 > 260 but 254 ≤ 260, 268 is an outlier and the right whisker ends at 254. [A1]

Only 268 is an outlier; the whiskers end at 142 and 254.

A dataset has lower quartile Q_1 = 35 and upper quartile Q_3 = 53. Calculate the upper outlier fence Q_3 + 1.5 × IQR.

  1. First find IQR = Q_3 - Q_1 = 53 - 35.
  2. Multiply IQR = 18 by 1.5 and add the result to Q_3 = 53.
IQR = 53 − 35 = 18
Upper fence = 53 + 1.5(18) = 53 + 27 = 80

Comparing two distributions with box plots

To compare two box plots, comment on a measure of centre (compare the medians) and a measure of spread (compare the IQRs or ranges), and say what each comparison means in context. For example: “Class B has the higher median, so Class B generally scored higher; Class A has the larger IQR, so the middle 50% of Class A's scores are more spread out.”

SL 4.3

Measures of center, standard deviation and linear coding

For a dataset with values x_1, x_2, ..., x_k occurring with frequencies f_1, f_2, ..., f_k (where n = Σ f_i), the mean x̄ and population standard deviation σ measure the center and typical distance from the mean:

x̄ = ∑ fi xin    |    σ = √∑ fi(xi − x̄)2n = √∑ fi xi2n − x̄2
xi
data value (or mid-interval value for grouped data)
fi
frequency of class i, with total sample size n = Σ f_i
σ, σ2
population standard deviation and population variance

GDC Trap — Always read σx, never sx: When you run 1-Var Stats on your GDC, it displays two standard deviations: sx (which divides by n - 1) and σx (which divides by n). In IB Math AA (both SL and HL), the standard deviation of a dataset is defined as σx. For grouped tables, enter the class midpoints in L1 and the frequencies in L2 (FreqList: L2).

Linear coding: shifting by +b vs scaling by ×a

Suppose every value x_i in a dataset is transformed by the linear rule y_i = a x_i + b (for example, converting temperatures from Celsius to Fahrenheit or rescaling exam marks):

  • Measures of position (mean, median, mode, Q_1, Q_3) are multiplied by a and shifted by +b: ȳ = a x̄ + b. If a < 0 the order reverses: the new Q_1 is aQ_3 + b and the new Q_3 is aQ_1 + b.
  • Measures of spread (standard deviation σ, IQR, range) are multiplied by |a| and are completely unaffected by +b: σ_y = |a|σ_x and IQR_y = |a|IQR_x.
  • Variance σ^2 is multiplied by a^2 (and unaffected by +b): σ_y^2 = a^2 σ_x^2.

Paper 1 linear coding and finding ∑x² from mean and variance

A sample of n = 10 measurements x_1, x_2, ..., x_{10} has mean x̄ = 6 and standard deviation σ_x = 2.
(a) Find the mean, standard deviation, and variance of the coded dataset y_i = 20 - 3x_i. [3 marks]
(b) Find the value of ∑_{i=1}^{10} x_i^2. [3 marks]

  1. The mean follows the full linear rule, whereas standard deviation scales by |−3| = +3 (never negative!).

    ȳ = 20 − 3x̄ = 20 − 3(6) = 2 [A1]
    σy = |−3|σx = 3 × 2 = 6, σy2 = 62 = 36 [A1 A1]

  2. Writing variance in terms of the mean of the squares lets us solve directly for ∑x².

    σx2 = ∑ xi210 − x̄2 ⇒ 22 = ∑ xi210 − 62 [M1 A1]

  3. Add 36 to both sides and multiply by n = 10.

    ∑ xi210 = 4 + 36 = 40 ⇒ ∑ xi2 = 400 [A1]

(a) ȳ = 2, σ_y = 6, σ_y² = 36; (b) ∑x² = 400

A dataset X has mean 15 and standard deviation σ_X = 3. Every value is transformed by Y = 100 - 4X. Find the standard deviation σ_Y of the transformed dataset.

  1. Adding 100 shifts the data without changing its spread.
  2. Multiplying by -4 scales the standard deviation by |-4| = 4.
σY = |−4| × σX = 4 × 3 = 12
SL 4.4, 4.10

Correlation and the two least-squares regression lines

Pearson's product-moment correlation coefficient r measures how closely bivariate data points (x_i, y_i) cluster around a straight line:

  • r = +1: perfect positive linear correlation; r = -1: perfect negative linear correlation; r = 0: no linear correlation.
  • Rough guide to strength (a rule of thumb only; IB does not define fixed cut-offs): 0.75 ≤ |r| ≤ 1 (strong), 0.5 ≤ |r| < 0.75 (moderate), |r| < 0.5 (weak).
  • Linear coding on r: Rescaling x or y by positive multipliers leaves r unchanged. Multiplying one variable by a negative constant flips the sign of r.

Interpreting the gradient a and the intercept b

On a scatter diagram, a line of best fit drawn by eye should pass through the mean point (x̄, ȳ). In the exam you find r and the regression line y = ax + b from the raw data on your GDC (LinReg(ax+b)), and then interpret the parameters in context:

  • Gradient a: the estimated change in y for each increase of one unit in x (for example, “each extra hour of revision is associated with about a more marks”).
  • Intercept b: the estimated value of y when x = 0. It is only meaningful if x = 0 lies within the range of the data; otherwise it is an extrapolation and should not be interpreted.

Why there are two different regression lines (y on x vs x on y)

When neither x nor y is controlled in an experiment (for example, height x and arm span y of students), there are two least-squares regression lines:

y on x:   y − ȳ = a(x − x̄)   (y = ax + b)    |    x on y:   x − x̄ = c(y − ȳ)   (x = cy + d)
y on x
minimizes squared vertical distances; use to estimate y from a given x
x on y
minimizes squared horizontal distances; use to estimate x from a given y
(x̄, ȳ)
the mean point where both regression lines always intersect

Extension (beyond the AA guide). Why the two lines only coincide when |r| = 1: The gradient of the y on x line is a = r(σ_y / σ_x), while the coefficient of y in the x on y line is c = r(σ_x / σ_y). Multiplying them gives a · c = r^2! Therefore, the two lines are identical if and only if r^2 = 1 (r = ±1), and they always cross at the mean point (x̄, ȳ). The identities a = r(σ_y / σ_x) and a · c = r^2 are not in the AA guide or the formula booklet: in the exam, r comes from your GDC.

Using both regression lines to find the mean point and predict x from y

For a bivariate dataset (x, y) with 10 ≤ x ≤ 30 and 15 ≤ y ≤ 45, the regression line of y on x is y = 1.2x + 6 and the regression line of x on y is x = 0.6y + 2.
(a) Find the mean values x̄ and ȳ. [3 marks]
(b) (Extension, beyond the AA guide) Find Pearson's correlation coefficient r. [2 marks]
(c) Estimate x when y = 25, and explain why your estimate is reliable. [3 marks]

  1. Both regression lines pass through the mean point (x̄, ȳ).

    Substitute ȳ = 1.2x̄ + 6 into x̄ = 0.6ȳ + 2: [M1]
    x̄ = 0.6(1.2x̄ + 6) + 2 = 0.72x̄ + 5.6 ⇒ 0.28x̄ = 5.6 ⇒ x̄ = 20 [A1]
    ȳ = 1.2(20) + 6 = 30 [A1]

  2. Extension only: in the exam r comes from the GDC. Since a = 1.2 > 0 and c = 0.6 > 0, the correlation is positive and r² = a · c.

    r2 = 1.2 × 0.6 = 0.72 ⇒ r = +√0.72 ≈ 0.849 (or 3√25) [M1 A1]

  3. Because y = 25 is given and x is being predicted, we must use the x on y line.

    x = 0.6(25) + 2 = 17 [M1 A1]
    Reliable because r ≈ 0.849 indicates strong positive correlation AND y = 25 lies within [15, 45] (interpolation). [R1]

(a) x̄ = 20, ȳ = 30; (b) r ≈ 0.849; (c) x = 17 (reliable by strong correlation and interpolation)

For a bivariate dataset, the regression line of y on x is y = 2x + 10 and the regression line of x on y is x = 0.4y + 8. Calculate the value of ȳ (the mean of y).

  1. Both lines intersect at (x̄, ȳ), so substitute x̄ = 0.4ȳ + 8 into ȳ = 2x̄ + 10.
  2. Solve ȳ = 2(0.4ȳ + 8) + 10 = 0.8ȳ + 26 for ȳ.
ȳ = 2(0.4ȳ + 8) + 10 = 0.8ȳ + 26
0.2ȳ = 26 ⇒ ȳ = 130
