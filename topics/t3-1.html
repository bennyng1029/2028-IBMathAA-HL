3D geometry, triangle trigonometry and radians

Guiding questionHow do we measure distances, angles, and areas in three-dimensional solids, non-right triangles, and circular sectors?

What you need to be able to do

Tick each item once you can do it confidently without checking your notes. Everything on this page is SL core (tested on both SL and HL papers).

  • Find the distance d and midpoint M between two points in 3D space (x_1, y_1, z_1) and (x_2, y_2, z_2).
  • Calculate the volume and surface area of right pyramids, right cones, spheres, hemispheres, and composite 3D solids.
  • Find the angle between a line and a plane (or between two planes) in a 3D solid by dropping a perpendicular to form a right-angled triangle.
  • Apply the sine rule, cosine rule, and triangle area formula A = (1/2)ab sin C in non-right-angled triangles.
  • Recognize and solve the ambiguous case (SSA) of the sine rule using both theta and 180° - theta (or pi - theta).
  • Draw and solve diagrams involving angles of elevation, angles of depression, and three-figure true bearings (000° to 360° clockwise from North).
  • Convert between degrees and radians (pi rad = 180°) and compute arc length l = r*theta, sector area A = (1/2)r^2*theta, chord length, and circular segment area.

Question decoder: spotting which technique to use

Geometry and trigonometry questions on IB exams rarely tell you which rule to use. Instead, they give you a diagram or a word clue. Use this table to translate exam phrasing directly into your first line of working.

“Ship B is on a bearing of 135° from A” Draw a North arrow at A (the “from” point) and measure 135° clockwise. Parallel North arrows at A and B form co-interior angles adding to 180°. Write final bearings with 3 digits (e.g. 042°, not 42°). The back-bearing from B to A differs by 180°.

Three-dimensional space, solids and angles with a plane

Three-dimensional coordinate geometry extends the 2D Pythagoras and average formulas by adding a third coordinate z. Think of (x, y) as your position on the floor of a room and z as your height above the floor.

Volumes and surface areas of 3D solids

The Prior Learning page of your IB Formula Booklet gives the volume and curved surface area formulas for right pyramids, right cones, and spheres. Notice the pattern: any solid that tapers to a sharp apex (a pyramid or a cone) has volume equal to one-third of its base area times its perpendicular height h.

Angle between a line and a plane: Imagine shining a torch straight down from above the plane. The angle between a line and a plane is the angle between the line and its shadow (orthogonal projection) on the plane. Always drop a perpendicular from the top of the line to the plane to build a right-angled triangle.

Height, volume and edge-to-base angle in a right square-based pyramid

A right pyramid VABCD has a horizontal square base ABCD of side length 10 cm. Each sloping edge VA, VB, VC, VD has length 13 cm. Let M be the centre of the square base.

(a) Find the exact length of AM, and hence find the vertical height VM. [3 marks]
(b) Calculate the volume of the pyramid, giving your answer to 3 significant figures. [2 marks]
(c) Find the angle that the edge VA makes with the base ABCD. [2 marks]

  1. The shadow of the edge VA on the horizontal base is the half-diagonal AM, from corner A to the centre M.

    AC = √(102 + 102) = √200 = 10√2 cm ⇒ AM = 10√22 = 5√2 cm [A1]

  2. Because the pyramid is a right pyramid, the apex V is directly above M, so ∠VMA = 90°.

    VM = √(VA2 − AM2) = √(132 − (5√2)2) = √(169 − 50) = √119 cm (≈ 10.91 cm) [M1 A1]

  3. The base is a 10 cm by 10 cm square with area A = 100 cm².

    V = 13 × 100 × √119 ≈ 363.623… = 364 cm3 (3 s.f.) [M1 A1]

  4. In right-angled triangle VMA, AM is adjacent to ∠VAM and VA = 13 cm is the hypotenuse.

    cos(∠VAM) = 5√213 ≈ 0.54393 ⇒ ∠VAM = arccos5√213 ≈ 57.0° (or 0.996 rad) [M1 A1]

(a) AM = 5√2 cm, VM = √119 cm ≈ 10.9 cm; (b) 364 cm³; (c) 57.0°

Angle between two planes vs angle between a line and a plane: In the pyramid above, the angle between the slant edge VA and the base is angle VAM (using the half-diagonal AM = 5 sqrt(2)). If a question asks for the angle between the sloping triangular face VAB and the base, you must drop a perpendicular from V and M to the midpoint N of side AB (where MN = 5 cm) and find angle VNM!

A rectangular box (cuboid) has dimensions 6 cm by 10 cm by sqrt(89) cm. Calculate the exact length of its longest internal space diagonal (from one bottom corner to the opposite top corner).

  1. By the 3D distance formula (3D Pythagoras), the space diagonal of a box with sides l, w, h is d = sqrt(l^2 + w^2 + h^2).
  2. Substitute 6^2 + 10^2 + (sqrt(89))^2 = 36 + 100 + 89 and take the square root.
d = √(62 + 102 + (√89)2) = √(36 + 100 + 89) = √225 = 15 cm

Triangle trigonometry, the ambiguous case and bearings

In any triangle ABC, we label the side opposite angle A as a, opposite B as b, and opposite C as c. Three formulas in your Formula Booklet let you solve any triangle once you know three pieces of information (including at least one side).

asin A = bsin B = csin C,    c2 = a2 + b2 − 2ab cos C,    Area = 12ab sin C
a, b, c
side lengths opposite interior angles A, B, C
cos C
rearranged form: cos C = (a2 + b2 − c2) / (2ab)

Why the Sine Rule has an ambiguous case (SSA)

Because sin(180° - theta) = sin(theta), a positive value of sin C corresponds to two angles between 0° and 180°: an acute angle C_1 = arcsin(k) and its obtuse supplement C_2 = 180° - C_1. Geometrically, if you fix angle A and adjacent side b, the minimum perpendicular distance from vertex C to the base line is h = b sin A. When the opposite side a satisfies b sin A < a < b, a compass of radius a centred at C cuts the base line in two different places!

Finding both possible triangles in an SSA problem

In triangle ABC, AB = 10 cm, BC = 7 cm, and angle BAC = 35°.
(a) Find the two possible values of angle ACB, giving your answers to 1 decimal place. [4 marks]
(b) Find the positive difference between the two possible lengths of side AC. [3 marks]

  1. We know an opposite pair (∠A = 35° and BC = a = 7 cm) and side AB = c = 10 cm opposite ∠ACB.

    sin C10 = sin 35°7 ⇒ sin C = 10 sin 35°7 ≈ 0.81939 [M1 A1]
    C1 = arcsin(0.81939…) = 55.0° (55.024° unrounded) [A1]

  2. Since a = 7 < c = 10, the supplementary angle C₂ = 180° − C₁ also leaves room for angle A = 35° (125.0° + 35° = 160.0° < 180°).

    C2 = 180° − 55.024° = 125.0° (124.976° unrounded) [A1]

  3. Method 1 uses the two values of ∠B = 180° − 35° − C. Method 2 sets up the Cosine Rule with b = AC: 7² = 10² + b² − 2(10)b cos 35°, which is b² − (20 cos 35°)b + 51 = 0!

    B1 = 180° − 35° − 55.024° = 89.976° ⇒ AC1 = 7 sin 89.976°sin 35° ≈ 12.204 cm [M1]
    B2 = 180° − 35° − 124.976° = 20.024° ⇒ AC2 = 7 sin 20.024°sin 35° ≈ 4.179 cm [A1]
    Difference = 12.204 − 4.179 = 8.03 cm (3 s.f.) [A1]

(a) 55.0° or 125.0°; (b) 8.03 cm

Exam shortcut for the difference between the two possible third sides: In any ambiguous SSA triangle with known angle A, adjacent side c, and opposite side a, the two possible third sides b_1, b_2 are the roots of the Cosine Rule quadratic b^2 - (2c cos A)b + (c^2 - a^2) = 0. By Vieta's root relations, the sum of the two possible lengths of AC is simply b_1 + b_2 = 2c cos A, and when the two sides a form an isosceles triangle at the base, b_1 - b_2 = 2a cos C_1!

Three-figure bearings and angles of elevation/depression

  • A three-figure true bearing is always measured from North (000°), turning clockwise, and written with three digits (such as 065° or 245°). Always look for the word “from”: the bearing of B from A is measured at A.
  • An angle of elevation (looking up) and an angle of depression (looking down) are always measured from the horizontal—never from a vertical wall or mast. Because horizontal lines are parallel, the angle of elevation of B from A equals the angle of depression of A from B (alternate interior angles).

In triangle PQR, PQ = 9 cm, QR = 8 cm, and the obtuse angle angle PQR satisfies cos(angle PQR) = -1/2. Find the area of triangle PQR to 3 significant figures.

  1. If cos(angle PQR) = -1/2, then angle PQR = 120° (or use sin(angle PQR) = sqrt(1 - (-1/2)^2) = sqrt(3)/2).
  2. Substitute into Area = (1/2) * PQ * QR * sin(angle PQR) = (1/2) * 9 * 8 * (sqrt(3)/2).
sin(∠PQR) = √32 ⇒ Area = 12(9)(8)√32 = 18√3 ≈ 31.2 cm2
SL 3.4

Radian measure, arcs, sectors and circular segments

Degrees (360° in a circle) are a historical convention. In higher mathematics and calculus, we measure angles in radians: one radian is the angle subtended at the centre of a circle of radius r by an arc of length r. Since the circumference of a full circle is 2*pi*r, a full turn of 360° is 2*pi radians, so pi rad = 180°.

l = rθ,    Asector = 12r2θ,    Asegment = 12r2(θ − sin θ)
r
radius of the circle
θ
central angle measured in radians (theta = deg * pi / 180)
l
arc length subtended by angle θ
Asegment
area between chord and arc: sector area (1/2)r^2*theta minus triangle area (1/2)r^2 sin(theta)

What is in the Formula Booklet vs what you must derive: Your booklet gives l = r*theta and A = (1/2)r^2*theta. It does not print the circular segment formula A_segment = (1/2)r^2(theta - sin(theta)) or the chord length formula AB = 2r sin(theta/2) = r*sqrt(2 - 2 cos(theta))—you construct these by subtracting or solving the isosceles triangle OAB with two sides of length r.

Perimeter and area of a circular segment

A circle with centre O and radius 8 cm has points A and B on its circumference such that angle AOB = 1.5 radians.
(a) Find the perimeter of the minor segment bounded by the chord AB and the minor arc AB, to 3 significant figures. [4 marks]
(b) Find the area of this minor segment, to 3 significant figures. [3 marks]

  1. Since θ = 1.5 is already in radians, multiply directly by r = 8 cm.

    l = rθ = 8 × 1.5 = 12 cm [A1]

  2. Triangle OAB is isosceles with OA = OB = 8 cm and ∠AOB = 1.5 rad. Use the Cosine Rule or bisect ∠AOB into two right triangles: AB = 2r sin(θ/2).

    AB = 2(8) sin(0.75) ≈ 16 × 0.681639 = 10.9062 cm [M1 A1]
    Perimeter of segment = l + AB = 12 + 10.9062 = 22.9 cm (3 s.f.) [A1]

  3. The minor segment is what remains of sector OAB after removing triangle OAB.

    Asegment = 12r2θ − 12r2 sin θ = 12(8)2(1.5 − sin 1.5) [M1 A1]
    Asegment = 32(1.5 − 0.997495) ≈ 16.1 cm2 (3 s.f.) [A1]

(a) 22.9 cm; (b) 16.1 cm²

Perimeter of a sector vs perimeter of a segment: Read carefully! The perimeter of a sector includes the two straight radii (P_sector = 2r + r*theta), whereas the perimeter of a segment is the arc plus the chord (P_segment = r*theta + 2r sin(theta/2)).

A sector of a circle of radius r = 6 cm has total perimeter 19.5 cm. Find the central angle theta in radians.

  1. The perimeter of a sector consists of two radii plus the arc length: P = 2r + r*theta.
  2. Substitute r = 6 into 12 + 6*theta = 19.5 and solve for theta.
2(6) + 6θ = 19.5 ⇒ 6θ = 7.5 ⇒ θ = 1.25 rad
