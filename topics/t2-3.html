Topic 2.3 · SL 2.11 + AHL 2.12–2.16

Transformations, polynomials and modulus functions

Guiding questionHow can we transform any graph systematically, extract polynomial roots and symmetries without expanding everything, and solve rational and modulus inequalities without losing solutions?

What you need to be able to do

  • Apply translations, reflections, and vertical/horizontal stretches—including composite transformations y = a f(b(x - h)) + k—in the correct order and track key coordinates.
  • Use the Remainder Theorem (P(b/a)) and Factor Theorem (P(k) = 0) to factorise polynomials and find unknown coefficients.
  • Use Vieta's formulas for the sum and product of polynomial roots to evaluate symmetric expressions like alpha^2 + beta^2 without solving for individual roots.
  • Sketch rational functions (ax + b)/(cx^2 + dx + e) and (ax^2 + bx + c)/(dx + e), finding vertical, horizontal, and oblique asymptotes via polynomial division.
  • Test for even (f(-x) = f(x)), odd (f(-x) = -f(x)), and self-inverse (f(f(x)) = x) functions, and restrict a domain at a turning point so f^{-1} exists.
  • Sketch y = |f(x)|, y = f(|x|), y = 1/f(x), and y = [f(x)]^2, and solve polynomial, rational, and modulus inequalities analytically and graphically.

Question decoder: spotting transformation, polynomial & inequality cues

Exam questions in Topic 2.3 reward students who spot the algebraic shortcut immediately—especially when the question forbids finding roots explicitly or hides an asymptote inside an improper fraction.

Question decoder — Transformations, polynomials, rational functions & modulus
What the exam question says What it means (formula & setup) Paper 1 (No GDC) vs Paper 2 (GDC)
“Describe the sequence of transformations from f(x) to a f(bx + c) + d” Factor inside f first: a f(b(x + c/b)) + d. Horizontal stretch by 1/b then shift by -c/b; vertical stretch by a then shift by d. Check any point (x_0, y_0) using coordinate tracking: x_new = (x_0 - c)/b and y_new = a y_0 + d.
“P(x) is divisible by (x - k)” / “(x - k) is a factor of P(x)” Apply the Factor Theorem: set P(k) = 0. Substitute x = k directly into P(x)—much faster than polynomial long division.
“Find the remainder when P(x) is divided by (ax - b)” Apply the Remainder Theorem: remainder is P(b/a). If divided by a quadratic (x - p)(x - q), write P(x) = Q(x)(x - p)(x - q) + mx + c and plug in x = p and x = q.
“Find alpha^2 + beta^2 (or 1/alpha + 1/beta) without finding the roots” Use Vieta's formulas for alpha + beta = -b/a and alpha beta = c/a, then rewrite alpha^2 + beta^2 = (alpha + beta)^2 - 2 alpha beta. For cubics, use sum alpha = -a_2/a_3, sum alpha beta = a_1/a_3, alpha beta gamma = -a_0/a_3.
“Find the equation of the oblique asymptote of (ax^2 + bx + c)/(dx + e)” Divide numerator by denominator: f(x) = mx + k + R/(dx + e). As x -> +-infty, remainder -> 0. The oblique asymptote is the linear quotient y = mx + k (must be written as y = …).
“Find the largest domain containing x = a for which f^{-1} exists” f must be one-to-one: cut the domain at the turning point where f'(x) = 0 (or vertex). On Paper 2, find the local max/min on the GDC; pick the branch around x = a up to the turning point.
“Sketch |f(x)| vs f(|x|) vs 1/f(x)” |f(x)|: reflect y < 0 up. f(|x|): copy x >= 0 to x < 0. 1/f(x): roots -> VAs, y = +-1 invariant. Always label x-intercepts, cusps, asymptotes, and reciprocal extrema (a, 1/M).
“Solve (ax + b)/(cx + d) >= k” or “|2x - 1| < |x + 3|” Rational: subtract k to one side, combine into one fraction, use a sign diagram. Two moduli: square both sides. Never cross-multiply by (cx + d)! On Paper 2, check your intervals against a GDC plot.
SL 2.11

Graph transformations and composite order

Every transformation of y = f(x) belongs to one of two independent worlds: vertical changes (outside f, affecting y directly) and horizontal changes (inside f, affecting x in the opposite direction).

Core graph transformations (SL 2.11)
Equation Geometric transformation Effect on point (x_0, y_0)
y = f(x) + k Vertical translation by vector (0, k) (x_0, y_0 + k)
y = f(x - h) Horizontal translation by vector (h, 0) (h > 0 moves right) (x_0 + h, y_0)
y = -f(x) Reflection in the x-axis (x_0, -y_0)
y = f(-x) Reflection in the y-axis (-x_0, y_0)
y = a f(x) Vertical stretch of scale factor |a| (plus x-axis reflection if a < 0) (x_0, a y_0)
y = f(bx) Horizontal stretch of scale factor 1/|b| (plus y-axis reflection if b < 0) (x_0 / b, y_0)

Composite transformations: why order matters

When both a stretch and a translation act in the same direction—for example, y = a f(bx + c) + d—the safest method is to factor b out inside the bracket first:

y = a f(b(x − h)) + k  ⇒  (x0, y0) ↦ (x0b + h,  ay0 + k)
Factored horizontal order
1. Horizontal stretch by factor 1/b (and reflect in y-axis if b < 0), then 2. Horizontal shift by h.
Vertical order
1. Vertical stretch by factor a (and reflect in x-axis if a < 0), then 2. Vertical shift by k.
Unfactored f(bx + c)
If you do not factor out b, you must shift by -c first, then stretch by 1/b (since x_new = (x_0 - c)/b).

Common mistake — Wrong horizontal shift in f(2x - 6): Students often see - 6 and state a translation of 6 units right after stretching by 1/2. Always factor first: f(2x - 6) = f(2(x - 3)), so the translation is 3 units right, not 6!

Finding the image of a point and describing composite transformations

The graph of y = f(x) has a local maximum at P(-4, 6). Let g(x) = -2 f(3x + 6) + 1.
(a) Describe a sequence of transformations that maps y = f(x) onto y = g(x). [4 marks]
(b) Find the coordinates of the image of P on y = g(x). [2 marks]

  1. Factoring b = 3 reveals the true horizontal translation h = −2 once the stretch is applied first.

    g(x) = −2 f(3(x + 2)) + 1   [M1]

  2. In factored form, stretches/reflections come before translations in each direction.

    1. Horizontal stretch of scale factor 13 (parallel to the x-axis)   [A1]
    2. Translation of 2 units left: vector (-2, 0)   [A1]
    3. Vertical stretch of scale factor 2 and reflection in the x-axis, followed by translation 1 unit up: vector (0, 1)   [A1]

  3. Check both by applying the steps and by solving 3x_new + 6 = −4.

    xnew = −43 − 2 = −103   [A1]
    ynew = −2(6) + 1 = −11   (now a local minimum!)   [A1]

(a) H-stretch 1/3, shift (-2, 0), V-stretch 2 + x-axis reflection, shift (0, 1); (b) (-10/3, -11)

The point (6, -4) lies on the graph of y = f(x). Find the y-coordinate of its image on the graph of y = -3 f(2x - 4) - 1.

  1. Remember that only the outside parameters a = -3 and k = -1 affect the y-coordinate.
  2. Compute y_new = -3(-4) - 1.
ynew = −3(−4) − 1 = 12 − 1 = 11 (and xnew = 6/2 + 2 = 5, giving (5, 11)).
AHL 2.12

Polynomial theorems, root multiplicity & Vieta's formulas

When a polynomial P(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1} x^{n-1} + ... + a_1 x + a_0 (a_n != 0) is divided by a linear divisor (ax - b), we can write P(x) = Q(x)(ax - b) + R. Substituting x = b/a eliminates Q(x) immediately:

  • Remainder Theorem: When P(x) is divided by (ax - b), the remainder is P(b/a). In particular, dividing by (x - k) leaves remainder P(k).
  • Factor Theorem: (ax - b) is a factor of P(x) if and only if P(b/a) = 0.
  • Root multiplicity & graph behaviour: At a single root (x - r)^1, the graph crosses the x-axis; at a double root (x - r)^2, it is tangent to the x-axis (bounces); at a triple root (x - r)^3, it crosses with a horizontal point of inflexion.

In your Formula Booklet (Section 2.12): For a polynomial equation sum_{r=0}^n a_r x^r = 0 of degree n, the sum of roots is -a_{n-1} / a_n and the product of roots is (-1)^n a_0 / a_n.

a3x3 + a2x2 + a1x + a0 = 0  ⇒  α + β + γ = −a2a3,   αβ + βγ + γα = +a1a3,   αβγ = −a0a3
Squared sum
alpha^2 + beta^2 + gamma^2 = (alpha + beta + gamma)^2 - 2(alpha beta + beta gamma + gamma alpha)
Reciprocal sum
1/alpha + 1/beta + 1/gamma = (alpha beta + beta gamma + gamma alpha) / (alpha beta gamma)

Common mistake — Sign of the product of roots: The formula has (-1)^n a_0 / a_n. For an even degree (quadratic, quartic), the product is +a_0 / a_n. For an odd degree (cubic), it is -a_0 / a_n. Always alternate signs -, +, -, +, ... starting from the x^{n-1} term—and watch for missing terms with coefficient 0!

Using Vieta's formulas and the Remainder Theorem together

The cubic equation 2x^3 - 5x^2 + kx - 6 = 0 has roots alpha, beta, gamma.
(a) Given that (x - 2) is a factor of P(x) = 2x^3 - 5x^2 + kx - 6, find k. [2 marks]
(b) Hence find the exact value of alpha^2 + beta^2 + gamma^2 without solving for all three roots. [4 marks]

  1. Since (x − 2) is a factor, substituting x = 2 must give 0.

    P(2) = 2(8) − 5(4) + 2k − 6 = 0 ⇒ 16 − 20 − 6 + 2k = 0 ⇒ k = 5   [M1 A1]

  2. Alternate signs (−, +, −) with leading coefficient a₃ = 2.

    α + β + γ = −−52 = 52   [A1]
    αβ + βγ + γα = +52   [A1]

  3. Expanding (α + β + γ)² isolates α² + β² + γ² plus twice the pairwise products.

    α2 + β2 + γ2 = (52)2 − 2(52) = 254 − 5 = 54   [M1 A1]

(a) k = 5; (b) 5/4 (or 1.25)

The polynomial P(x) = x^3 + ax^2 - 7x + b leaves a remainder of 6 when divided by (x - 1). Find the value of a + b.

  1. By the Remainder Theorem, the remainder when P(x) is divided by (x - 1) is P(1).
  2. Set P(1) = 1^3 + a(1)^2 - 7(1) + b = 6 and solve for a + b.
P(1) = 1 + a − 7 + b = 6 ⇒ a + b − 6 = 6 ⇒ a + b = 12.

Not needed for IB: You do not need formal proofs of the Rational Root Theorem or Descartes' Rule of Signs. On Paper 1, any root needed to start factorising a cubic or quartic is either a small integer (+-1, +-2, +-3) or guided by the question.

AHL 2.13 · AHL 2.14

Higher rational functions, oblique asymptotes & symmetry

Beyond the linear-over-linear hyperbolas of SL 2.8, AHL 2.13 covers two richer rational families:

AHL 2.13 rational function families and asymptotes
Family Vertical asymptotes (VA) Horizontal / Oblique asymptote
f(x) = (ax + b) / (cx^2 + dx + e) Solve cx^2 + dx + e = 0 (0, 1, or 2 VAs; check if numerator is also 0 for a hole). Denominator has higher degree, so horizontal asymptote is always y = 0.
f(x) = (ax^2 + bx + c) / (dx + e) Set dx + e = 0 => x = -e/d (1 VA, unless (dx + e) divides numerator). Numerator degree is 1 higher: divide to get mx + k + R/(dx + e). Oblique asymptote y = mx + k.

Even, odd, self-inverse & domain restriction (AHL 2.14)

  • Even function: f(-x) = f(x) for all x in the domain (symmetric about the y-axis; e.g., x^2, cos x, |x|).
  • Odd function: f(-x) = -f(x) for all x in the domain (rotational symmetry of order 2 about the origin; e.g., x^3, sin x, 1/x).
  • Self-inverse function: f(f(x)) = x, meaning f^{-1}(x) = f(x) and the graph is symmetric about y = x.
  • Restricting a domain for f^{-1}: A function has an inverse if and only if it is one-to-one (passes the horizontal line test). For a many-to-one curve, cut the domain at its turning point(s) where f'(x) = 0.

Exam tip — Finding the range of (ax^2 + bx + c)/(dx + e) without calculus: Set y = f(x), clear the denominator to form a quadratic in x, and require Delta_x >= 0 (since x in R). Solving the resulting inequality in y gives the exact range!

Finding an oblique asymptote and range of a quadratic-over-linear function

Consider f(x) = (2x^2 - 3x + 5)/(x - 2) for x != 2.
(a) Express f(x) in the form mx + k + R/(x - 2) and state the equation of the oblique asymptote. [3 marks]
(b) Use the discriminant method to find the range of f. [4 marks]

  1. Synthetic or polynomial long division separates the linear quotient from the decaying remainder.

    2x2 − 3x + 5 = (2x + 1)(x − 2) + 7   [M1]
    f(x) = 2x + 1 + 7x − 2   [A1]
    Oblique asymptote: y = 2x + 1   (as x → ±∞, 7/(x − 2) → 0)   [A1]

  2. A value y lies in the range if and only if there is at least one real x producing it.

    y(x − 2) = 2x2 − 3x + 5 ⇒ 2x2 − (3 + y)x + (5 + 2y) = 0   [M1]

  3. Real x exists iff the discriminant in x is non-negative.

    Δx = (3 + y)2 − 8(5 + 2y) = y2 − 10y − 31 ≥ 0   [M1 A1]
    Roots: y = 10 ± √(100 + 124)2 = 5 ± √56 = 5 ± 2√14
    Range: y ≤ 5 − 2√14 or y ≥ 5 + 2√14   [A1]

(a) y = 2x + 1; (b) y <= 5 - 2 sqrt(14) or y >= 5 + 2 sqrt(14)

The rational function f(x) = (x^2 + 3x - 2)/(x + 4) has an oblique asymptote y = x + k. Find the exact value of k.

  1. Divide x^2 + 3x - 2 by (x + 4): start with x(x + 4) = x^2 + 4x.
  2. Subtracting x^2 + 4x leaves -x - 2 = -1(x + 4) + 2, so the quotient is x - 1.
f(x) = x − 1 + 2x + 4 ⇒ oblique asymptote is y = x − 1, so k = −1.

Not needed for IB: AHL 2.13 only examines linear / quadratic and quadratic / linear rational functions. Non-linear (parabolic) asymptotes from cubic / linear are outside the IB syllabus.

AHL 2.15 · AHL 2.16

Modulus, reciprocal & squared graphs and inequalities

Given the graph of y = f(x), you must be able to sketch four derived graphs and solve inequalities involving them:

Derived graph transformations (AHL 2.16)
Transformation Rule to sketch from y = f(x) Key features to label
y = |f(x)| Keep f(x) >= 0 unchanged; reflect any part with f(x) < 0 upwards in the x-axis. Sharp corners (cusps) at simple x-intercepts; no negative y-values.
y = f(|x|) Delete the left half (x < 0); reflect the right half (x >= 0) across the y-axis. Always an even graph; cusp at (0, f(0)) unless f'(0) = 0.
y = 1/f(x) Invert y-values: roots become VAs; VAs become x-intercepts; y -> +-infty becomes y -> 0. Invariant points at y = +-1; local max (a, M) becomes local min (a, 1/M).
y = [f(x)]^2 Square all y-values: non-negative everywhere; values in (0, 1) shrink, |y| > 1 grow. Invariant points at y = 0 and y = 1; simple roots become smooth tangent minima.

Common mistake — Cross-multiplying rational inequalities: Never multiply (2x - 1)/(x + 3) >= 1 by (x + 3) because x + 3 can be negative (which flips the inequality) or zero! Always move everything to one side (LHS - RHS >= 0), combine into a single fraction, and use a sign diagram—or multiply by (x + 3)^2 > 0.

Solving a rational inequality and a modulus inequality

Solve the following inequalities analytically:
(a) (2x - 1)/(x + 3) >= 1 [3 marks]
(b) |2x - 3| < |x + 1| [4 marks]

  1. Keeping everything in one fraction preserves the sign of the denominator (x + 3).

    2x − 1x + 3 − 1 ≥ 0 ⇒ (2x − 1) − (x + 3)x + 3 ≥ 0 ⇒ x − 4x + 3 ≥ 0   [M1 A1]
    Critical values: x = −3 (excluded, VA) and x = 4 (included). Sign diagram (+, −, +) gives x < −3 or x ≥ 4   [A1]

  2. Both sides are non-negative, so squaring preserves the inequality and removes both absolute value bars.

    (2x − 3)2 < (x + 1)2 ⇒ 4x2 − 12x + 9 < x2 + 2x + 1   [M1]
    3x2 − 14x + 8 < 0 ⇒ (3x − 2)(x − 4) < 0   [M1 A1]
    Upward parabola is < 0 between the roots: 23 < x < 4   [A1]

(a) x < -3 or x >= 4; (b) 2/3 < x < 4

The graph of y = f(x) has a local minimum at (3, 0.1). Find the y-coordinate of the corresponding local maximum on the graph of y = 1/f(x).

  1. For y = 1/f(x), a positive local minimum at (a, m) becomes a local maximum at (a, 1/m).
  2. Compute 1 / 0.1.
ymax = 10.1 = 10 at x = 3.
