Counting, partial fractions and extended binomials

Guiding questionWhen does order change a count, how can we split a rational expression into simpler fractions, and when does an infinite binomial series converge?

What you need to be able to do

Everything on this page is Additional HL (AHL 1.10–1.11). Tick each skill when you can do it cleanly without looking at your notes.

  • Distinguish between ordered arrangements (nPr) and unordered selections (nCr), and solve algebraic equations involving factorials.
  • Solve constrained counting problems using the glue/block method (“together”), gap or complement method (“never together”), and complement (“at least one”).
  • Decompose rational expressions with at most two distinct linear factors into partial fractions, including improper quadratics over two linear factors.
  • Expand (1 + x)^n and (a + bx)^n for negative or fractional indices n ∈ ℚ by factoring out a^n first.
  • State the exact interval of validity |x| < |a / b|, and take the intersection of intervals when combining partial fractions.

AHL 1.10

Counting principles, permutations and combinations

Every counting question starts with one decision: does order matter? If swapping two chosen items creates a different outcome (like a 4-digit PIN or a seating plan), you are counting permutations. If swapping them leaves the outcome unchanged (like a committee or a hand of cards), you are counting combinations.

Section 1.10 of your Formula Booklet gives the permutation formula alongside the combination formula from Section 1.9:

nPr = n!(n − r)!, nCr = n!r!(n − r)! = nPrr!
n
total number of distinct items available
r
number of items chosen or arranged (0 <= r <= n)
r!
number of ways to order those r chosen items

When problems add constraints, use the three core IB strategies:

  • Glue (Block) method for “must be together”: wrap the k items into one super-item, arrange the reduced set of objects, and multiply by k! for arrangements inside the block.
  • Gap (Insertion) method for “never together”: arrange the m unrestricted items first in m! ways. This creates m + 1 gaps (including both ends). Place the k separated items into k distinct gaps in {}^{m+1}P_k ways.
  • Complement method for “at least one” or “2 items not together”: subtract the forbidden count from the unconstrained total.

Two classic counting traps:
1. Never use Total - (all k together) when k >= 3 items must be separated! If 3 vowels must not be adjacent, subtracting “all 3 together” still leaves arrangements where 2 vowels sit side by side. Always use the Gap method for 3 or more separated items.
2. Never double-count in “at least” selections: picking 1 required engineer in 5C1 ways and then picking 2 more people from the remaining 8 candidates counts pairs of engineers twice. Split into mutually exclusive exact counts (or use the complement).

What is NOT needed for IB: Permutations of indistinct objects (n! / (n_1! n_2!)), Stars-and-Bars ((n+k-1)C(k-1)), and derangements are not required formulas in the IB AA HL syllabus.

Arranging books with a separation constraint (Gap method)

Four distinct mathematics books and three distinct physics books are placed on a shelf. Find the number of arrangements in which no two physics books are adjacent. [5 marks]

  1. Arranging the 4 mathematics books creates fixed slots around them where physics books can sit safely.

    4! = 24 ways [M1]

  2. 4 books create 5 gaps: _ M _ M _ M _ M _. Since the physics books are distinct, order matters when filling 3 of the 5 gaps.

    5P3 = 5 × 4 × 3 = 60 ways [M1 A1]

  3. By the multiplication principle, combine the math arrangements with the gap placements.

    24 × 60 = 1440 [M1 A1]

1440

A 5-person committee is chosen from 6 physicists and 4 mathematicians. How many different committees contain at least 3 physicists?

  1. Split into mutually exclusive exact cases: (3 physicists, 2 mathematicians), (4 physicists, 1 mathematician), and (5 physicists, 0 mathematicians).
  2. Compute 6C3 × 4C2 + 6C4 × 4C1 + 6C5 × 4C0.
6C34C2 + 6C44C1 + 6C54C0 = (20 × 6) + (15 × 4) + (6 × 1) = 120 + 60 + 6 = 186
AHL 1.11

Partial fractions

Before you can integrate a rational function or expand it as a binomial series, you often need to split a single fraction with a factored denominator into a sum of simpler fractions. This reverse-addition process is called partial fraction decomposition.

px + q(ax + b)(cx + d) = Aax + b + Bcx + d
px2 + qx + r(x − a)(x − b) = K + Ax − a + Bx − b (improper: divide first!)

Always check the degree first!
If the numerator’s degree is greater than or equal to the denominator’s degree (e.g. quadratic over quadratic), you must do polynomial division (or split off the leading constant K = p / (ac)) before writing A / (ax + b) + B / (cx + d).

Fastest Paper 1 method (Heaviside substitution): Multiply both sides by the denominator to get px + q ≡ A(cx + d) + B(ax + b), then substitute the root of each linear factor (x = -b/a and x = -d/c). One bracket vanishes each time, giving A and B in two lines.

What is NOT needed for IB: In IB Math AA HL (1.11), partial fractions are restricted to denominators with at most two distinct linear factors. You do not need repeated linear factors (A/(x-a) + B/(x-a)^2) or irreducible quadratic factors ((Ax+B)/(x^2+c^2)), even though AI tools often include them.

Decomposing an improper rational expression into partial fractions

Express (x^2 + 6x - 1) / ((x - 1)(x + 2)) in the form A + B / (x - 1) + C / (x + 2). [5 marks]

  1. Multiply both sides by (x − 1)(x + 2) so we can find A, B, and C cleanly.

    x2 + 6x − 1 ≡ A(x − 1)(x + 2) + B(x + 2) + C(x − 1) [M1]

  2. Only A(x − 1)(x + 2) produces an x² term on the right-hand side.

    A = 1 [A1]

  3. Choosing the roots of the linear factors eliminates the other unknowns immediately.

    x = 1: 1 + 6 − 1 = 3B ⇒ 6 = 3B ⇒ B = 2 [M1 A1]
    x = −2: 4 − 12 − 1 = −3C ⇒ −9 = −3C ⇒ C = 3 [A1]

1 + 2 / (x − 1) + 3 / (x + 2)

Given that (7x - 1) / ((x - 1)(x + 2)) = A / (x - 1) + B / (x + 2), find the value of A.

  1. Write 7x - 1 = A(x + 2) + B(x - 1).
  2. Substitute x = 1 so the B term vanishes.
At x = 1: 7(1) − 1 = A(1 + 2) ⇒ 6 = 3A ⇒ A = 2
AHL 1.10 & 1.11

The extended binomial theorem for rational indices

In Topic 1.2, (a + b)^n with n ∈ ℤ+ always stopped after n + 1 terms. When n is negative or fractional (n ∈ ℚ), the expansion never terminates—it becomes an infinite series that only converges when the variable term has magnitude strictly less than 1.

Section 1.10 of your Formula Booklet gives the extended binomial series for n ∈ ℚ and |x| < 1:

(1 + x)n = 1 + nx + n(n − 1)2!x2 + n(n − 1)(n − 2)3!x3 + … (|x| < 1, n ∈ ℚ)
(a + bx)n = an(1 + bax)n valid for |bax| < 1 ⇔ |x| < |ab|

Three common extended-binomial mistakes:
1. Forgetting a^n when factoring: (4 - x)^{-1/2} = 4^{-1/2}(1 - x/4)^{-1/2} = (1/2)(1 - x/4)^{-1/2}, not 4(1 - x/4)^{-1/2}.
2. Dropping negative signs inside u: in (1 - 3x)^{-2}, u = -3x, so u^2 = +9x^2.
3. Taking the union instead of the intersection of validity intervals: if one partial fraction is valid for |x| < 1 and the other for |x| < 2, their sum is only valid where both converge: |x| < 1.

Partial fractions followed by an extended binomial expansion

Express f(x) = (5x + 1) / ((1 - x)(2 + x)) in partial fractions, and hence find the first three terms of its expansion in ascending powers of x. State the set of values of x for which the expansion is valid. [6 marks]

  1. Splitting into two linear denominators turns f(x) into two (a + bx)⁻¹ terms we can expand separately.

    5x + 1 ≡ A(2 + x) + B(1 − x) [M1]
    x = 1 ⇒ 6 = 3A ⇒ A = 2; x = −2 ⇒ −9 = 3B ⇒ B = −3 [A1 A1]
    f(x) = 2(1 − x)−1 − 3(2 + x)−1

  2. Factor 2⁻¹ = 1/2 out of (2 + x)⁻¹ before applying the (1 + u)⁻¹ formula.

    2(1 − x)−1 = 2(1 + x + x2 + …) = 2 + 2x + 2x2 + … (valid for |x| < 1)
    −3(2 + x)−1 = −32(1 + x2)−1 = −32(1 − x2 + x24 − …) = −32 + 34x − 38x2 + … (valid for |x| < 2) [M1]

  3. Add the coefficients term by term, and choose the stricter convergence condition.

    f(x) ≈ 12 + 114x + 138x2, valid for |x| < 1 [A1 R1]

1/2 + (11/4)x + (13/8)x², valid for |x| < 1

Find the coefficient of x^2 in the binomial expansion of (4 - x)^{-1/2} (enter an exact fraction like 3/256 or a 3 s.f. decimal).

  1. Factor out 4^{-1/2} = 1/2 first: (1/2)(1 - x/4)^{-1/2}.
  2. The x^2 term inside the bracket is ((-1/2)(-3/2)/2)(-x/4)^2 = (3/8)(x^2/16) = (3/128)x^2. Multiply by 1/2.
12 × (-1/2)(-3/2)2! × (−14)2 = 12 × 38 × 116 = 3256 (≈ 0.0117)
