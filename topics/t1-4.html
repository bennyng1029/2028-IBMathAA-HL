Complex numbers and De Moivre's theorem

Guiding questionHow does extending the real number line into the two-dimensional Argand plane turn algebraic powers and roots into geometric rotations and regular polygons?

What you need to be able to do

Before this page: radians, the unit circle and exact trigonometric values (3.1 and 3.2), the compound-angle identities (3.2, AHL 3.10), geometric series (1.1), the binomial theorem (1.2) and proof by induction (1.5).

  • Add, subtract, multiply and divide complex numbers in Cartesian form z = a + bi, and identify Re(z) = a and Im(z) = b.
  • Use the complex conjugate z* = a - bi and the property z z* = |z|^2 = a^2 + b^2 to rationalize complex denominators and solve equations.
  • Convert between Cartesian form a + bi, modulus-argument (polar) form r(cos theta + i sin theta) = r cis theta, and Euler form r e^{i theta} with correct quadrant adjustment for arg(z).
  • Interpret complex multiplication and division geometrically on the Argand diagram as scaling of moduli and rotation of arguments.
  • Prove De Moivre's theorem [r(cos theta + i sin theta)]^n = r^n(cos(n theta) + i sin(n theta)) by induction for n \in \mathbb{Z}^+, and apply it to compute powers and derive multiple-angle trigonometric identities.
  • Find all n distinct n-th roots of a complex number z^n = w and represent them as the vertices of a regular n-gon on the Argand diagram.
  • Apply the Complex Conjugate Root Theorem to factorize and solve polynomial equations with real coefficients.

AHL 1.12

Cartesian form, conjugates and the Argand diagram

To solve equations like x^2 + 1 = 0 that have no real solutions, we define the imaginary unit i by the property i^2 = -1. Every complex number z \in \mathbb{C} can be written in Cartesian form:

z = a + bi,    z* = a − bi,    zz* = |z|2 = a2 + b2
a = Re(z)
real part of z (a real number)
b = Im(z)
imaginary part of z (a real number, without i)
z*
complex conjugate of z (reflection of z across the real axis)

Never include i in Im(z)! For z = 5 - 3i, the imaginary part is the real number Im(z) = -3, not -3i. Writing Im(z) = -3i loses the accuracy mark on Paper 1 and Paper 2.

Powers of i and arithmetic in Cartesian form

Because i^2 = -1, powers of i repeat in a cycle of length 4: i^1 = i, i^2 = -1, i^3 = -i, i^4 = 1. To simplify i^n, divide the exponent n by 4 and look only at the remainder.

  • Addition and subtraction: Combine real parts and imaginary parts separately: (a + bi) +- (c + di) = (a +- c) + (b +- d)i.
  • Multiplication: Expand brackets and replace i^2 with -1: (a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i.
  • Division (rationalizing the denominator): Multiply both numerator and denominator by the complex conjugate of the denominator c - di so the denominator becomes the real number c^2 + d^2: (a + bi) / (c + di) = ((a + bi)(c - di)) / (c^2 + d^2).
  • Equating real and imaginary parts: Two complex numbers are equal if and only if both their real parts and their imaginary parts match: a + bi = c + di <=> a = c and b = d (provided a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{R}).
  • Quadratics with a negative discriminant: If a real quadratic has Delta = b^2 - 4ac < 0, the quadratic formula gives two complex conjugate roots. For example, z^2 - 4z + 13 = 0 gives z = (4 +- sqrt(-36)) / 2 = (4 +- 6i) / 2 = 2 +- 3i.

The Argand diagram

On the Argand diagram (complex plane), z = a + bi is plotted as the point (a, b) or position vector (a, b)^T, with the horizontal axis for Re(z) and the vertical axis for Im(z). Taking the conjugate z^* = a - bi reflects z across the horizontal real axis, while addition z_1 + z_2 follows standard head-to-tail vector addition.

Complex division and equating real and imaginary parts

Let z_1 = 2 + 3i and z_2 = 1 - 2i.

(a) Express w = z_1 / z_2 in the form a + bi, where a, b \in \mathbb{Q}. [3 marks]

(b) Find the real numbers p and q such that (p + qi) z_2 = 7 + 4i. [3 marks]

  1. The denominator is z₂ = 1 − 2i, so its conjugate is z₂* = 1 + 2i. Multiplying by (1 + 2i)/(1 + 2i) turns the denominator into 1² + (−2)² = 5.

    w = (2 + 3i)(1 + 2i)(1 − 2i)(1 + 2i) [M1]
    w = 2 + 4i + 3i + 6i212 + (−2)2 = (2 − 6) + 7i5 = −45 + 75i [A1 A1]

  2. Since p and q are real numbers, we can expand the left-hand side into (Real) + (Imaginary)i and equate to 7 + 4i.

    (p + qi)(1 − 2i) = (p + 2q) + (q − 2p)i = 7 + 4i [M1]

  3. Setting Re(LHS) = 7 and Im(LHS) = 4 gives two linear equations in p and q.

    p + 2q = 7 and −2p + q = 4 [M1]
    2(p + 2q) + (−2p + q) = 14 + 4 ⇒ 5q = 18 ⇒ q = 185, p = −15 [A1]

(a) −4/5 + (7/5)i    (b) p = −1/5, q = 18/5

Given that z = (11 + 3i) / (1 - 2i), express z in the form a + bi and enter the value of a + b.

  1. Multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate 1 + 2i. The denominator becomes 1^2 + (-2)^2 = 5.
  2. Expand the numerator: (11 + 3i)(1 + 2i) = 11 + 22i + 3i + 6i^2 = 5 + 25i. Now divide both parts by 5.
z = (11 + 3i)(1 + 2i)12 + (−2)2 = 5 + 25i5 = 1 + 5i
a + b = 1 + 5 = 6
AHL 1.13

Modulus-argument (polar) and Euler forms

Instead of describing a point on the Argand plane by its horizontal and vertical coordinates (a, b), we can describe it by its distance from the origin and the angle it makes with the positive real axis:

  • The modulus r = |z| = sqrt(a^2 + b^2) >= 0 is the distance from the origin to z.
  • The argument theta = arg(z) is the directed angle from the positive real axis to z. Unless a question states otherwise, IB uses the principal argument in the interval -pi < theta <= pi.

In your Formula Booklet (AHL 1.12–1.13): Since a = r cos theta and b = r sin theta, and by Euler's formula e^{i theta} = cos theta + i sin theta, every complex number can be written in three equivalent ways:

z = a + bi = r(cos θ + i sin θ) = r cis θ = reiθ

Finding the principal argument arg(z) \in (-\pi, \pi]

When a != 0 and b != 0, first find the acute reference angle alpha = arctan(|b / a|) in (0, pi/2), then plot (a, b) in its quadrant to assign the correct sign or shift. A point on an axis needs no arctan (last row):

Quadrant adjustment for principal argument θ = arg(a + bi) ∈ (−π, π]
Quadrant of (a, b) Signs of a, b Principal argument theta = arg(z) Example
Quadrant I a > 0, b > 0 theta = +alpha 1 + sqrt(3)i => r = 2, theta = pi/3
Quadrant II a < 0, b > 0 theta = pi - alpha -1 + i => r = sqrt(2), theta = 3pi/4
Quadrant III a < 0, b < 0 theta = -(pi - alpha) = alpha - pi -sqrt(3) - i => r = 2, theta = -5pi/6
Quadrant IV a > 0, b < 0 theta = -alpha 1 - i => r = sqrt(2), theta = -pi/4
On an axis a = 0 or b = 0 Positive real: 0; positive imaginary: pi/2; negative real: pi; negative imaginary: -pi/2 arg(-3) = pi, arg(2i) = pi/2, arg(-2i) = -pi/2

Never trust arctan(b/a) without sketching the quadrant! For z = -1 - i (Quadrant III), b/a = (-1)/(-1) = 1, so arctan(1) = pi/4 (Quadrant I). The true principal argument is arg(-1 - i) = -3pi/4.

Geometric meaning of complex multiplication and division

Using exponent laws on Euler form z_1 = r_1 e^{i theta_1} and z_2 = r_2 e^{i theta_2} immediately shows why polar/Euler form is so powerful:

z1z2 = r1r2ei(θ1 + θ2)     and     z1z2 = r1r2ei(θ1 − θ2)
|z1z2| = |z1||z2|
moduli multiply (and divide under division)
arg(z1z2) = θ1 + θ2
arguments add (modulo 2π) under multiplication and subtract under division

Geometrically, multiplying any complex number z_1 by z_2 = r_2 e^{i theta_2} stretches its length by a scale factor of r_2 and rotates it anticlockwise about the origin by angle theta_2. In particular, multiplying by i = e^{i pi/2} is a pure anticlockwise rotation by 90^\circ (pi/2 radians).

Finding exact trigonometric values by comparing Cartesian and Euler forms

Let z_1 = 1 + i and z_2 = sqrt(3) - i.

(a) Express z_1 and z_2 in Euler form r e^{i theta}. [3 marks]

(b) Hence, by expressing z_1 z_2 in both Cartesian and Euler forms, find the exact value of cos(pi/12) and sin(pi/12). [5 marks]

  1. z₁ = 1 + i is in Quadrant I with r₁ = √(1² + 1²) = √2 and θ₁ = π/4. z₂ = √3 − i is in Quadrant IV with r₂ = √(3 + 1) = 2 and θ₂ = −π/6.

    z1 = √2 eiπ/4 [A1]
    z2 = 2 e−iπ/6 [M1 A1]

  2. In Euler form, multiply moduli and add arguments: π/4 − π/6 = π/12. In Cartesian form, expand (1 + i)(√3 − i).

    Euler: z1z2 = 2√2 ei(π/4 − π/6) = 2√2 eiπ/12 = 2√2 (cos(π/12) + i sin(π/12)) [M1]
    Cartesian: (1 + i)(√3 − i) = (√3 + 1) + (√3 − 1)i [A1]

  3. Both expressions represent the exact same complex number z₁z₂, so their real and imaginary parts must be equal.

    2√2 cos(π/12) = √3 + 1 ⇒ cos(π/12) = √3 + 12√2 = √6 + √24 [M1 A1]
    2√2 sin(π/12) = √3 − 1 ⇒ sin(π/12) = √3 − 12√2 = √6 − √24 [A1]

cos(π/12) = (√6 + √2)/4,   sin(π/12) = (√6 − √2)/4

Find the principal argument arg(z) (in radians) of the complex number z = -3 sqrt(3) + 3i. You may enter an exact multiple of pi (such as 3*pi/4) or a 3 s.f. decimal.

  1. Identify the quadrant: Re(z) = -3 sqrt(3) < 0 and Im(z) = 3 > 0, so z lies in Quadrant II.
  2. The acute reference angle is alpha = arctan(3 / (3 sqrt(3))) = arctan(1 / sqrt(3)) = pi/6. In Quadrant II, theta = pi - pi/6 = 5pi/6.
α = arctan(1 / √3) = π / 6
Quadrant II: arg(z) = π − π / 6 = 5π / 6 (≈ 2.62 rad)
AHL 1.14

De Moivre's theorem, n-th roots and polynomial roots

Repeatedly multiplying z = r(cos theta + i sin theta) by itself n times multiplies the modulus to r^n and adds the angle n times to n theta. This result is known as De Moivre's theorem. It is proved by induction for n \in \mathbb{Z}^+ (worked example below) and is also true for all n \in \mathbb{Z}:

[r(cos θ + i sin θ)]n = rn(cos nθ + i sin nθ) = rn cis nθ = rneinθ

For a rational power n = p/q, the formula gives only one of the q values of z^{p/q}; the others come from replacing theta by theta + 2 pi k (see n-th roots below).

Proving De Moivre's theorem by mathematical induction

Prove by mathematical induction that (cos theta + i sin theta)^n = cos(n theta) + i sin(n theta) for all n \in \mathbb{Z}^+. [6 marks]

  1. Check that both sides agree when n = 1.

    For n = 1: LHS = cos θ + i sin θ = RHS, so P(1) is true. [R1]

  2. Assume the result for some positive integer k.

    Assume P(k) is true for some k ∈ ℤ+: (cos θ + i sin θ)k = cos kθ + i sin kθ. [M1]

  3. Split off one factor, use the hypothesis, expand, then use the compound angle identities for cos(A + B) and sin(A + B).

    (cos θ + i sin θ)k+1 = (cos kθ + i sin kθ)(cos θ + i sin θ) [M1]
    = (cos kθ cos θ − sin kθ sin θ) + i(sin kθ cos θ + cos kθ sin θ) [A1]
    = cos((k + 1)θ) + i sin((k + 1)θ), so P(k + 1) is true. [A1]

  4. Link the base case and the inductive step.

    Since P(1) is true, and P(k) being true implies P(k + 1) is true, by the principle of mathematical induction P(n) is true for all n ∈ ℤ+. [R1]

(cos θ + i sin θ)n = cos nθ + i sin nθ for all n ∈ ℤ⁺. Multiplying by rn gives the form with modulus r.

Euler bridge identities for trigonometric proofs (useful for Paper 3-style proofs): For a complex number on the unit circle z = cos theta + i sin theta = e^{i theta}, we have z^{-1} = cos theta - i sin theta = e^{-i theta}. By De Moivre's theorem:

z^n + z^{-n} = 2 cos(n theta)   and   z^n - z^{-n} = 2i sin(n theta).

Expanding (z + z^{-1})^n = (2 cos theta)^n expresses powers like cos^4 theta in terms of multiple angles cos(4 theta) and cos(2 theta) so they can be integrated directly!

Solving z^n = w: the n-th roots of a complex number

Every non-zero complex number w = r e^{i theta} has n distinct n-th roots. Because adding 2 pi k (k \in \mathbb{Z}) to the argument of w leaves w unchanged, writing w = r e^{i(theta + 2 pi k)} and taking the (1/n)-th power gives:

zk = r1/n exp(i θ + 2πkn) = r1/n cis(θ + 2πkn),    k = 0, 1, 2, …, n − 1
  • Regular n-gon geometry: All n roots have the exact same modulus r^{1/n} and are equally spaced around the circle of radius r^{1/n} by an angle of 2 pi / n radians. For n >= 3, they form the vertices of a regular n-gon centered at the origin, so the sum of all n roots is always 0.
  • Roots of unity (z^n = 1): Setting r = 1, theta = 0 gives the n-th roots of unity 1, omega, omega^2, ..., omega^{n-1} where omega = e^{2 pi i / n}, which satisfy 1 + omega + omega^2 + ... + omega^{n-1} = 0.

Complex Conjugate Root Theorem for real polynomials

If a polynomial P(z) = a_n z^n + a_{n-1} z^{n-1} + ... + a_1 z + a_0 has real coefficients (a_k \in \mathbb{R}) and z_1 = a + bi (b != 0) is a root of P(z) = 0, then its complex conjugate z_1^* = a - bi is also a root. Together, the conjugate pair produces a real quadratic factor of P(z):

(z − z1)(z − z1*) = z2 − (z1 + z1*)z + z1z1* = z2 − 2az + (a2 + b2) = z2 − (2r cos θ)z + r2

Not needed for IB Math AA HL: General complex loci on the Argand plane (such as Apollonius circles |z - z_1| = k|z - z_2| or half-line/arc loci arg((z - z_1)/(z - z_2)) = alpha), 2 × 2 matrix representations of complex numbers, and complex logarithms or trigonometric functions of complex inputs are not in the AA HL syllabus.

Finding all cube roots of a complex number and using conjugate pairs

(a) Solve the equation z^3 = -4 sqrt(2) + 4 sqrt(2)i, giving your answers in the form r e^{i theta} where r > 0 and -pi < theta <= pi. [5 marks]

(b) Given that z = 2 - i is a root of the real polynomial P(z) = z^3 - 7z^2 + 17z - 15 = 0, find the other two roots. [4 marks]

  1. First compute |w| and locate w in Quadrant II to get its principal argument, then add 2πk for the three roots.

    |w| = √((−4√2)2 + (4√2)2) = √(32 + 32) = √64 = 8 [A1]
    Quadrant II: arg(w) = π − π4 = 3π4 ⇒ z3 = 8 ei(3π/4 + 2πk) [A1]

  2. Taking the 1/3 power gives modulus 81/3 = 2 and angles (3π/4 + 2πk)/3 = π/4 + 2πk/3.

    r = 81/3 = 2,    θk = 3π/4 + 2πk3 = π4 + 2πk3 [M1]
    z0 = 2eiπ/4,   z1 = 2ei(11π/12),   z−1 = 2e−i(5π/12) [A1 A1]

  3. Because all coefficients of P(z) are real, the conjugate (2 − i)* = 2 + i is automatically a root, giving a quadratic factor z² − 4z + 5.

    Since coefficients are real, z = 2 + i is also a root. [R1]
    (z − (2 − i))(z − (2 + i)) = z2 − 4z + 5 [M1]
    z3 − 7z2 + 17z − 15 = (z2 − 4z + 5)(z − 3) = 0 ⇒ z = 3 [M1 A1]

(a) 2eiπ/4, 2e11πi/12, 2e−5πi/12    (b) 2 + i and 3

Let z = sqrt(3) + i. Find the smallest positive integer n such that z^n is a real number.

  1. Convert z = sqrt(3) + i to polar/Euler form: r = 2 and theta = arg(z) = pi/6.
  2. By De Moivre's theorem, z^n = 2^n(cos(n pi/6) + i sin(n pi/6)). For z^n to be real, we need Im(z^n) = 0 <=> sin(n pi/6) = 0.
z = 2eiπ/6 ⇒ zn = 2n(cos(nπ/6) + i sin(nπ/6))
Im(zn) = 0 ⇔ sin(nπ/6) = 0 ⇔ nπ/6 = kπ ⇒ least n ∈ ℤ+ is 6
