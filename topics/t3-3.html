Vectors, lines and planes in 3D space

Guiding questionHow do the dot product and cross product let us turn any 3D geometric question about angles, distances, lines, and planes into clean algebra?

What you need to be able to do

This entire topic is Additional Higher Level (AHL 3.12–3.18). Tick each item once you can solve it without checking your notes.

  • Find displacement vectors AB = b - a, magnitudes |v|, unit vectors v / |v|, and rescale a direction vector to a given speed.
  • Compute the scalar (dot) product v \cdot w = |v||w| cos theta, find the angle between two vectors, and test for perpendicularity (v \cdot w = 0).
  • Write vector (r = a + lambda*b), parametric, and Cartesian equations of a 3D line, and model constant-velocity motion r(t) = r_0 + t*v.
  • Classify a pair of 3D lines as parallel, coincident, intersecting, or skew, and find the shortest distance from a point to a line.
  • Compute the vector (cross) product v \times w to find a common perpendicular (normal) vector and calculate the area of a parallelogram |v \times w| or triangle (1/2)|v \times w|.
  • Convert between vector (r = a + lambda*b + mu*c), normal (r \cdot n = a \cdot n), and Cartesian (ax + by + cz = d) equations of a plane.
  • Find intersections and acute angles between a line and a plane (sin phi = |b \cdot n| / (|b||n|)) and between two or three planes.

AHL 3.12–3.13

3D vectors, unit vectors and the scalar (dot) product

A point A(a_1, a_2, a_3) in 3D space has position vector OA = a = a_1 i + a_2 j + a_3 k. The displacement vector from A to B is always “head minus tail”: AB = OB - OA = b - a.

|v| = √(v12 + v22 + v32),    v̂ = v|v|,    v · w = v1w1 + v2w2 + v3w3 = |v||w| cos θ
v̂
unit vector of length 1 in the direction of v; a vector of speed s in direction d is s * (d / |d|)
v · w
scalar (dot) product (returns a single number, never a vector!)
cos θ
cos theta = (v \cdot w) / (|v||w|); for non-zero vectors, v \cdot w = 0 <=> theta = 90°

Key algebraic properties of the dot product: Because cos 0° = 1, dotting any vector with itself gives the square of its magnitude: v \cdot v = |v|^2. Together with u \cdot v = v \cdot u and distributivity, you can expand |u + v|^2 = (u + v) \cdot (u + v) = |u|^2 + 2(u \cdot v) + |v|^2 just like ordinary algebra!

Using |u − v|² = (u − v) · (u − v) with given magnitudes

[Paper 1 — No GDC] Given that u and v are vectors with |u| = 3, |v| = 4, and |u - v| = sqrt(37), find the angle theta between u and v. [4 marks]

  1. Writing |u − v|² = (u − v) · (u − v) brings the dot product u · v into play.

    |u − v|2 = |u|2 − 2(u · v) + |v|2 [M1]

  2. We know |u − v|² = 37, |u|² = 9, and |v|² = 16.

    37 = 9 − 2(u · v) + 16 ⇒ 2(u · v) = 25 − 37 = −12 ⇒ u · v = −6 [A1]

  3. Substitute u · v = −6 and |u||v| = 12.

    cos θ = −63 × 4 = −12 ⇒ θ = 2π3 (or 120°) [M1 A1]

θ = 2π/3 (or 120°)

[Paper 1 — No GDC] Find the value of k such that the vectors u = (3, -2, k) and v = (2, 5, 2) are perpendicular.

  1. Two non-zero vectors are perpendicular if and only if their dot product is zero: u \cdot v = 0.
  2. Solve 3(2) + (-2)(5) + k(2) = 0.
6 − 10 + 2k = 0 ⇒ 2k = 4 ⇒ k = 2

Proving a geometric property with vectors

[Paper 1 — No GDC] OABC is a rhombus with \mathbf{OA} = \mathbf{a} and \mathbf{OC} = \mathbf{c}. Use vectors to show that the diagonals OB and AC are perpendicular. [4 marks]

  1. A rhombus is a parallelogram, so AB = OC = c. Then OB = OA + AB, and AC is "head minus tail".

    OB = a + c,    AC = c − a [M1 A1]

  2. Expand like ordinary algebra, using a · c = c · a so the middle terms cancel.

    (a + c) · (c − a) = c · c − a · a = |c|2 − |a|2 [M1]

  3. All four sides of a rhombus are equal, so |a| = |c|, and a zero dot product of non-zero vectors means they are perpendicular.

    |a| = |c| ⇒ (a + c) · (c − a) = 0 ⇒ OB ⊥ AC [R1] [AG]

(a + c) · (c − a) = 0, so the diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular.

AHL 3.14–3.15

Equations of lines in 3D, kinematics and skew lines

A straight line in 3D space is determined by a known point A(x_0, y_0, z_0) with position vector a and a non-zero direction vector b = (l, m, n):

r = a + λb  ⇔  x = x0 + λl, y = y0 + λm, z = z0 + λn  ⇔  x − x0l = y − y0m = z − z0n
a
position vector of any specific point on the line
b = (l, m, n)
direction vector parallel to the line (in kinematics, the velocity vector v)
λ (or t)
real scalar parameter (lambda in R); in kinematics, time t >= 0

Reading the direction vector from a Cartesian line equation: Before reading off (x_0, y_0, z_0) and (l, m, n), the coefficients of x, y, z in the numerators must all be +1! For example, (2 - x)/3 = (2y - 4)/6 = z must be rewritten as (x - 2)/(-3) = (y - 2)/3 = (z - 0)/1, giving point (2, 2, 0) and direction vector (-3, 3, 1).

Classifying two lines in 3D space

Unlike lines in a 2D plane, two non-parallel lines in 3D space do not have to intersect—they can pass over and under each other like a bridge over a highway (skew lines):

  1. Compare the direction vectors b_1 and b_2: If b_1 = k*b_2, the lines are parallel. Test if one line's starting point lies on the other line: if yes, they are coincident (the same line); if no, they are strictly parallel.
  2. If not parallel, equate coordinates using two distinct parameters lambda and mu: Solve the first two equations (x and y) for lambda and mu, then substitute into the third (z) equation. If the third equation holds, the lines intersect at a unique point; if it fails, the lines are skew.

Finding the foot of the perpendicular and shortest distance from a point to a line

Consider the point P(1, 5, 1) and the line L with vector equation r = (2, 1, 0) + lambda(1, 2, 2).
(a) Find the coordinates of the point F on L that is closest to P. [4 marks]
(b) Hence find the shortest distance from P to L. [2 marks]

  1. Any point F on L has coordinates (2 + λ, 1 + 2λ, 2λ), so PF = OF − OP.

    PF = (2 + λ − 1, 1 + 2λ − 5, 2λ − 1) = (1 + λ, 2λ − 4, 2λ − 1) [M1 A1]

  2. The shortest segment PF is perpendicular to the line's direction vector b = (1, 2, 2).

    (1 + λ)(1) + (2λ − 4)(2) + (2λ − 1)(2) = 0 ⇒ 9λ − 9 = 0 ⇒ λ = 1 [M1 A1]

  3. Plug λ = 1 into OF = (2 + λ, 1 + 2λ, 2λ) and PF = (1 + λ, 2λ − 4, 2λ − 1).

    F = (3, 3, 2),    PF = (2, −2, 1) ⇒ |PF| = √(22 + (−2)2 + 12) = √9 = 3 [M1 A1]

(a) F(3, 3, 2); (b) 3

Find the shortest distance from the point P(0, 4, 2) to the line r = (1, 0, 1) + lambda(1, 2, 2).

  1. Write PF = (1 + lambda, 2*lambda - 4, 2*lambda - 1) and set PF \cdot (1, 2, 2) = 0.
  2. You will find 9*lambda - 9 = 0 => lambda = 1, giving PF = (2, -2, 1).
λ = 1 ⇒ PF = (2, −2, 1) ⇒ |PF| = √(22 + (−2)2 + 12) = 3
AHL 3.16–3.18

The vector (cross) product, areas and planes in 3D space

Whereas the dot product of two vectors is a scalar, the vector (cross) product v \times w returns a 3D vector that is perpendicular to both v and w.

v × w = (v2w3 − v3w2)i + (v3w1 − v1w3)j + (v1w2 − v2w1)k,    |v × w| = |v||w| sin θ
Area of parallelogram
A = |v \times w| where v, w are adjacent sides
Area of triangle ABC
A = (1/2)|AB \times AC|
Plane equations
r = a + lambda*b + mu*c  ⇔  r \cdot n = a \cdot n  ⇔  n_1 x + n_2 y + n_3 z = d

Angles and intersections involving lines and planes (AHL 3.18)

A plane Pi is uniquely defined by a point on it and its normal vector n = (a, b, c) perpendicular to the surface. Because n stands at 90° to the plane, finding the acute angle phi between a line (direction b) and a plane (normal n) uses sine instead of cosine:

sin ϕ = |b · n||b||n|   (line and plane),      cos θ = |n1 · n2||n1||n2|   (two planes)
ϕ
acute angle between line direction b and the plane (complement of the angle between b and n)
θ
acute angle between two planes with normal vectors n1 and n2

Cartesian equation of a plane through three points and intersection with a line

Points A(1, 0, 2), B(2, 3, 1), and C(0, 1, 4) lie on a plane Pi.
(a) Find the area of triangle ABC, and find the Cartesian equation of the plane Pi. [5 marks]
(b) Find the coordinates of the point where the line L: r = (1, 5, 0) + lambda(1, -1, 1) intersects Pi, and find the acute angle between L and Pi. [5 marks]

  1. AB × AC gives a normal vector n perpendicular to the plane, and half its magnitude is the area of ΔABC.

    AB = (1, 3, −1),    AC = (−1, 1, 2) [A1]
    n = AB × AC = (3(2) − (−1)(1), (−1)(−1) − 1(2), 1(1) − 3(−1)) = (7, −1, 4) [M1 A1]

  2. Since |n| = √(49 + 1 + 16) = √66, Area = ½√66.

    Area = 12|AB × AC| = √662 (≈ 4.06),    7x − y + 4z = 7(1) − 0 + 4(2) = 15 [A1 A1]

  3. On L, x = 1 + λ, y = 5 − λ, z = λ.

    7(1 + λ) − (5 − λ) + 4(λ) = 15 ⇒ 12λ + 2 = 15 ⇒ λ = 1312 [M1 A1]
    Intersection point = (2512, 4712, 1312) [A1]

  4. Here b = (1, −1, 1) with |b| = √3 and n = (7, −1, 4) with |n| = √66.

    sin ϕ = |1(7) + (−1)(−1) + 1(4)|√3 √66 = 123√22 = 4√22 ≈ 0.8528 ⇒ ϕ ≈ 58.5° (or 1.02 rad) [M1 A1]

(a) Area = √66 / 2, plane: 7x − y + 4z = 15; (b) (25/12, 47/12, 13/12), ϕ ≈ 58.5°

Find the exact area of triangle OAB where O(0, 0, 0) is the origin, A(1, 2, 2), and B(2, 1, -2).

  2. The area of triangle OAB is (1/2)|OA \times OB| = (1/2)sqrt((-6)^2 + 6^2 + (-3)^2).
